# Meditations of a Discount Marcus: The First-Move Tax

*In which a philosopher of questionable qualifications attempts to understand why two adults matching on an application apparently requires one of them to organize a diplomatic mission.*

---

<!-- truncate -->

Somewhere in Eastern Europe, civilization had advanced far enough to invent smartphones, dating applications, artificial intelligence, and the ability to communicate instantly with another human being on the opposite side of the planet.

Remarkable achievements, really.

And yet, somehow, the act of two people saying hello to one another remained a logistical challenge roughly equivalent to Hannibal crossing the Alps.

Enter Helen.

Forty years old. Interested in meeting new people, making friends, traveling, drinking, listening to jazz, dancing salsa, and enjoying a good shisha.

Splendid.

A woman who apparently enjoys experiences, conversations, and human connection.

And there I was, another human being, inconveniently available for precisely those activities.

On November 3, we matched.

A mutual expression of interest. Two adults, both equipped with functioning thumbs, smartphones, and presumably enough cognitive capacity to construct a sentence.

Then came the silence.

One day.

Two days.

Nothing.

Not even a fucking pigeon carrying a note.

Discount Marcus observed the situation with the detached wisdom of a man who had spent fifty years watching humanity invent increasingly sophisticated methods of accomplishing absolutely nothing.

*"Interesting,"* he thought. *"Two individuals have voluntarily indicated an interest in communicating, and both now occupy the same digital space without communicating. Perhaps this is a new form of meditation."*

But Marcus knew better.

He had encountered this particular ritual before.

The Great Eastern European Courtship Ceremony.

An ancient tradition in which a woman expresses interest, and the man must subsequently demonstrate his worthiness by composing the opening sentence of what may or may not become a conversation.

A sentence that must be confident, but not arrogant.

Funny, but not childish.

Charming, but not manipulative.

Interested, but not desperate.

Original, but not weird.

Masculine, but not intimidating.

Respectful, but not boring.

And ideally delivered at precisely the right moment, under a favorable lunar alignment, with the blessing of at least three deceased ancestors.

Marcus considered these requirements.

*"For fuck's sake,"* he concluded.

## The Emperor Makes His Move

On November 5, having allowed sufficient time for the Roman Senate to deliberate, I finally sent Helen a message.

Not a pickup line.

Not a carefully rehearsed introduction.

Not some ridiculous declaration about how her smile illuminated the darkness of my otherwise miserable existence.

No.

I simply acknowledged the absurdity.

"Well Helen here we are. You were waiting for me to make the first move. Now I have."

And then, because apparently we were participating in an exceptionally dangerous social experiment:

"I know... talking to strangers online is scary."

There.

Done.

A magnificent diplomatic achievement.

The first move had been made.

The empire could sleep peacefully.

Somewhere, a thousand dating coaches collapsed from disappointment.

There had been no carefully calibrated compliment. No strategic emotional manipulation. No mysterious question designed to trigger curiosity.

Just a fifty-year-old man being a sarcastic asshole and thoroughly enjoying himself.

Discount Marcus approved.

*"You have power over your mind, not over external events,"* he reminded me.

Then, after examining the message, he added:

*"Although you apparently still have to initiate the fucking events."*

## Equality, Terms and Conditions Apply

Now, here's where my philosophical irritation begins.

We live in a society that talks endlessly about equality.

Independence.

Empowerment.

Freedom from traditional gender roles.

Beautiful words.

I happen to agree with the underlying principle.

Two adults. Equal dignity. Equal agency. Equal freedom to pursue whatever the hell they want.

Wonderful.

But then we arrive at the dating application.

And suddenly the calendar reads 1826.

The man approaches.

The man initiates.

The man entertains.

The man demonstrates confidence.

The man finds something clever to say.

The man carries the conversation until such time as the woman decides whether the performance deserves continued attention.

What happened to all that independence?

Did it expire when the notification appeared?

Was there a fucking software update that disabled the Send button on half the population's phones?

And this isn't merely about Helen. I don't know her circumstances, and I certainly don't know what she was thinking.

She might have been busy.

She might have forgotten.

She might have had twelve other conversations.

She might simply not have wanted to message first.

Fine.

I'm talking about the recurring ritual I've encountered, particularly here in Eastern Europe, where modern language about equality sometimes sits comfortably beside expectations that would make a nineteenth-century aristocrat feel right at home.

I spent years living elsewhere, and returning to this part of the world has made some of these expectations feel especially antiquated.

A hundred years behind?

Two hundred?

Hard to say.

The architecture occasionally suggests the former Soviet Union, while the social rituals occasionally suggest the fucking Roman Empire.

At least the Romans were honest about having an emperor.

## An Audition Nobody Asked For

What irritates me most isn't even having to write first.

Writing a message takes seconds.

I've written entire software systems with less hesitation than some people apparently experience composing "Hello."

It's the underlying expectation.

The notion that one person must perform while the other evaluates.

That I must somehow earn the privilege of participating in an ordinary conversation.

And God forbid the opening line isn't quite right.

Too direct.

Too sarcastic.

Too strange.

Too ordinary.

Too much personality.

Not enough personality.

Apparently, before speaking to another human being, I must consult a panel of experts, commission a feasibility study, establish appropriate performance indicators, and submit my personality for regulatory approval.

Discount Marcus, who could barely afford his own toga, found this economically unsustainable.

*"If a man must become a comedian, diplomat, psychologist, entertainer, and motivational speaker simply to say hello,"* he observed, *"perhaps the position should come with a salary."*

Precisely.

I'm not applying for employment.

I'm not auditioning for a television show.

I'm not selling a fucking timeshare.

We're two people who matched on an application.

We can talk.

Or not.

It's really that simple.

## The Art of Being Ridiculous

And this is where things have changed for me.

There was a time when I might have spent more energy wondering how a message would be received.

Should I phrase it differently?

Will she appreciate the sarcasm?

Will she think I'm being rude?

Could I make a better impression?

But now?

Fuck it.

I'm going to be ridiculous.

Not because I'm trying to shock anybody.

Not because I need to prove some grand philosophical principle.

And certainly not because I expect everyone to share my particular brand of humor.

I'm going to be ridiculous because I enjoy being ridiculous.

Because the absurdity of everyday human behavior genuinely entertains me.

And because I have absolutely no interest in manufacturing a more marketable version of myself for the approval of strangers.

If Helen laughs, wonderful.

If she doesn't, that's fine too.

If she responds with sarcasm of her own, we might actually have an entertaining conversation.

If she replies with a single-word answer and expects me to perform the next twelve acts of the circus, well, that will tell me something about the conversation.

And if she never responds?

The sun will rise.

The Earth will continue rotating.

The Roman Empire will remain inconveniently deceased.

And Discount Marcus will still have enjoyed his own joke.

## The Stoic Problem with Dating Applications

There is something almost comically Stoic about the whole situation.

The ancient philosophers spent enormous amounts of time explaining that we control our own judgments, intentions, and actions, but not the responses of other people.

And here we are, two thousand years later, with entire industries devoted to convincing people they can optimize precisely those responses.

Choose the right photograph.

Write the perfect biography.

Send the perfect message.

Wait the correct number of minutes.

Maintain the proper balance of emotional availability and manufactured indifference.

Apparently, human connection has become a marketing funnel.

Marcus Aurelius commanded armies and governed an empire.

Discount Marcus sits in Bulgaria, staring at a dating application and wondering why saying hello has become an exercise in strategic communications.

The decline of civilization is magnificent.

But here's the part I actually appreciate.

I made the first move without needing the outcome.

I said what amused me.

I expressed myself without tailoring every word to an imagined expectation.

The message is out there.

What happens next belongs to Helen.

And I don't need to occupy myself with it.

Not everything requires a strategy.

Not every interaction requires an investment of emotional energy.

Not every stranger needs to appreciate my personality.

Sometimes the entire achievement is simply refusing to make something unnecessarily complicated.

## A Final Note from the Discount Emperor

Perhaps Helen will respond.

Perhaps she won't.

Perhaps she'll turn out to be someone with a wonderful sense of humor and enough personality to make conversation effortless.

Perhaps we'll drink something, smoke shisha, listen to jazz, and exchange ridiculous stories about our lives.

Or perhaps nothing will happen at all.

Both possibilities are perfectly acceptable.

What isn't appealing to me anymore is the expectation that I should carry an entire interaction merely because I happen to be the man.

Equality isn't a ceremonial declaration.

It's something people demonstrate through their actions.

And I have no intention of demanding that someone act according to my preferences any more than I intend to reshape myself to satisfy theirs.

I can initiate.

I can make an effort.

I can be interested.

But I won't perform on command.

So, Helen, the opening move has been made.

The messenger has crossed the frontier.

The gates of Rome are open.

The Senate has adjourned.

And somewhere in the ruins of a once-great civilization, Discount Marcus raises a glass to the extraordinary technological progress that has enabled two adults to avoid saying hello with unprecedented efficiency.

**I can care deeply and detach completely.**

Although, in this particular case, I mostly just wanted to make a fucking joke.

And I did.

*— Discount Marcus, Emperor of Absolutely Fucking Nothing*
