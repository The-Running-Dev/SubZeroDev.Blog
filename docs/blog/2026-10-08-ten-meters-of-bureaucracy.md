# Ten Meters of Bureaucracy

Two guys walked into the bar.

One of them asked how I was. I said I was good.

Then he told me I was as good as they were.

"How the fuck do you know how good I am?"

He looked at me like I should know him. I looked at him like a man who had just made an unusually confident claim about a stranger's wellbeing.

<!-- truncate -->

Then he mentioned my aunt. And the car.

Oh. *That* car.

My relatives had left a car in the yard and put stones in front of it so it couldn't be moved. My mother wanted it moved. I helped arrange the tow crew, we cleared the stones, and the car ended up about ten meters away, out in front of the house.

Somehow this became a police matter. The complaint was about "self-help." I showed the police my mother's power of attorney and the ownership papers. I explained why the car had been moved and that it had been blocking access to the property.

They were still investigating me.

And now, months later, one of the people caught up in that ridiculous afternoon was standing in my bar, remembering me perfectly. I couldn't place him at all. I talk to a lot of people. Apparently some of them have been cast in episodes of my life without me learning their names.

I apologized that he'd had to go through it. My family made a mess and other people got dragged into it. He didn't deserve that.

The investigator remembers me too. When I saw her again, she said I had been a reasonable person. "Sort of," I thought. Meanwhile, I was trying to remember what she looked like and privately realizing she looked better than I remembered. I did not say that part out loud.

I have her number because of the investigation. If she summons me to the office again, I may ask whether she'd like to get a drink sometime. That seems about as sensible as anything else in this case.

The tow crew recognizes me at the bar. The investigator recognizes me at the station. Somewhere in Bulgaria, ten meters of road has generated more introductions than my entire social calendar.
