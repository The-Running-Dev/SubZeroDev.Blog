# Schadenfreude in the Plumbing Department

Somewhere along the way, I discovered that the German language contains a word so exquisitely suited to certain moments in life that I can't believe English has managed without it.

<!-- truncate -->

*Schadenfreude.*

A German coworker introduced me to it. Pleasure at somebody else's misfortune.

Magnificent word. Truly. I didn't know it existed until someone told me, and now my Bulgarian family has apparently volunteered to produce a demonstration.

The latest episode concerns water pipes.

First, the urgent message was about the cover over the place where the water gets shut off. You must put the top on, because water will get in.

All right. It gets full of water.

And then?

Well, presumably it drains.

But the subject has now expanded. Winter is coming. The pipes will freeze. They'll burst. Something terrible will happen, and therefore, apparently, everybody has something to say about whether I can keep living here.

These pipes are, to my understanding, a couple of meters underground. That doesn't make freezing physically impossible. Weather, exposed sections, connections, frost depth—fine. Reality can do what reality does.

What I'm not buying is the transformation of an eventuality into a family proclamation carved into stone.

I've seen this kind of thing repeatedly here. Somebody hears that something bad happened once, somewhere, to somebody. Before you know it, it has become an eternal rule, enforced by people who have never checked whether the circumstances are remotely the same.

Water exists. Winter exists. Therefore calamity is inevitable.

Thank you, Bulgarian Academy of Plumbing Folklore.

And the larger family dispute is still there, underneath every bit of this. I suspect the next move will be to point at the pipes and tell me the house is unsuitable for me to live in. They already dislike the fact that I am here; the frozen-pipe argument would be another convenient reason to say I should be somewhere else.

But I don't have to accept somebody else's forecast as a command.

I might stay long enough to see what actually happens. Maybe nothing freezes. Maybe something does. I'm not planning to damage the pipes, and I'm not under the illusion that water obeys my sarcasm.

I also have another place I can live.

And that's the wonderful thing about the entire drama. I am not trapped in some grand winter survival film, fighting the elements with my bare hands while the family's committee of elders chants about frost.

If the house becomes unlivable, I can leave.

The people who have been so determined to turn my presence into a problem can then handle the plumbing without me.

Now, I recognize that frozen pipes aren't intrinsically funny. Broken plumbing is a nuisance. It can cost money. It can make somebody's life miserable.

But the idea of people spending so much energy trying to use winter as an argument against me, only to inherit the actual winter problem themselves?

Oh, come on.

The Germans have a word for that.

*Schadenfreude.*

I wasn't looking for an opportunity to practice it.

The plumbing department simply submitted an application.
