Donald Trump is threatening Iran again, and people are understandably alarmed.

Me? I hear Chicken Little.

That's not an endorsement. That's not me saying the threat is harmless. It's me asking a question that gets lost every time the man opens his mouth: **how much weight do I give this particular proclamation, given the gap between what he says and what he actually does?**

<!-- truncate -->

## The Sky Is Falling. Again.

Canada. Greenland. Iran. The list of grand declarations is long enough that each new one starts to sound like an encore.

Some threats have been followed by action. Others haven't. And even when action follows, the scale and shape can be very different from the rhetoric that preceded it.

So no, I'm not saying, "Ignore the president of the United States." That would be ridiculous.

I'm saying **don't mistake a statement for a completed event**.

If somebody with enormous power repeatedly says extraordinary things, the repetition creates a strange problem. You still have to take the person seriously. You also have to decide whether the words are a useful prediction of what happens next.

I don't know whether a threat to annihilate Iran will be followed by anything resembling the words. I hope it won't. I don't pretend to know.

## Serious Is Not the Same as Literal

What fascinates me is how quickly the discussion turns into a false choice.

Either I must be panicking because the president said something horrifying, or I must be excusing him because I'm not panicking.

Fuck that.

I can object to the language and question whether the threat describes an actual plan. Both can be true.

A threat can be reckless without being a reliable forecast. Skepticism about a speaker's follow-through isn't approval of the threat.

That distinction should be ordinary. Somehow it becomes controversial.

## The American Part

I'm happy to be an American citizen. That's a legal reality I value. It isn't a declaration of national pride, and it isn't my identity. America is part of my history; it isn't the definition of me.

I've heard enough American political theater for one lifetime. I left the country. I still pay attention sometimes. I still have opinions.

But I don't owe every alarming headline the same emotional investment.

## The Part That Isn't Funny

The danger in the Chicken Little comparison is obvious: eventually something might actually fall.

That's why I don't dismiss the possibility of action. The power is real, and the consequences could be catastrophic.

But the question remains: when someone constantly talks up a bigger game than they deliver, why should each new sentence be treated as a literal timetable?

You can condemn the words, acknowledge the power behind them, and still ask that question.

That's my position. Not "the sky can't fall." Not "everything is fine."

Just: **the man is talking again. Let's distinguish the talking from the doing.**

Hopefully he doesn't do it.

Who the fuck knows?
