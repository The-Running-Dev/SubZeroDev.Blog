# The Mother Has Left the Chat

I was enjoying a perfectly ordinary afternoon in Hell when an email arrived that made me reconsider whether we were adequately funded.

<!-- truncate -->

“Father,” I called upstairs. “We need to discuss the family department.”

“What’s happened now?”

“Ben has written Elizabeth a goodbye email.”

God sighed.

“How long?”

“The goodbye?”

“The email.”

“Several decades.”

Discount Marcus looked up from a folding chair. He was wearing a bedsheet and holding a copy of *Meditations* with the receipt still inside.

“Remember,” he said, “we suffer more in imagination than in reality.”

“That’s Seneca, you clearance-rack bastard. And we have screenshots.”

---

The trouble with writing a final email is that you usually begin with the intention of making one point.

Then another point appears.

Then a childhood memory kicks the door open carrying supporting documentation.

Ben began politely.

> Elizabeth, thank you so much for your last email and for the opportunity to clarify my position.

I stopped reading.

“Oh, this is going to be fucking catastrophic.”

“Why?” asked God. “He said thank you.”

“Yes. In the opening sentence of a family email. That’s the diplomatic equivalent of seeing the catering staff evacuate an embassy.”

The clarification followed.

He was happier without having to deal with her.

He remembered his childhood.

He remembered the choices she had made, the principles the family had paid for, and the difference between their lives and the lives of her friends’ children.

One banana per child. Dog sausage and fries as a big occasion.

“He remembers the menu?” God asked.

“He was there, Father. People do occasionally retain information from their own lives.”

Then came Vassil.

Ben wrote about what he believed Elizabeth had done to his father: ground him down, diminished him, treated him as though he mattered less. He blamed her for the destruction he had watched.

And then, amid all that anger, came a detail small enough to fit on a business card.

**Kostadinov and Sons.**

The name his father had wanted for his welding company.

I read it twice.

“No elaborate speech,” I said. “A man thinking about a business name and putting his sons in it.”

Discount Marcus lowered his book.

For once, he had the sense to let a sentence sit.

---

There was one memory of Elizabeth acting like the mother Ben had wanted.

He was fifteen. A girl he liked. A conversation on a balcony in Stara Zagora.

One moment he could still name.

Then Syracuse. An award for being the best computer science student.

Where was she?

According to Ben’s email: nowhere.

“And Yavor doesn’t know these things,” I said.

“Was he included?”

“Oh, yes. Ben copied him in.”

God leaned back.

“The family distribution list.”

“Apparently some historical context had not reached all stakeholders.”

Discount Marcus raised a finger.

“Perhaps a meeting—”

“Finish that sentence and I’ll make you facilitate it.”

He lowered the finger.

Ben’s message to Yavor was direct. When Elizabeth wasn’t there, who had been?

Martin. Ben.

Now, as Ben described it, the relationship had become conditional on loyalty to Elizabeth.

He had a few fucking questions about that arrangement.

He also had a conclusion.

You are no longer my brother.

There was no calendar invitation attached.

---

The email was furious. Nobody needed a panel of experts to establish that.

It contained profanity, accusations, old memories, a wish that Elizabeth would face the loneliness Ben associated with his father’s ending, and an acknowledgement that he expected to face his own ending alone too.

He did not leave himself outside the consequences.

Then, after the fury, he wished her peace and tranquility in her final years.

“Quite a tonal range,” God observed.

“It’s a goodbye to his mother, Father. He’s allowed more than one fucking note.”

He said this was the last time she would hear from him.

He said goodbye.

He invoked God.

God looked at me.

“Why am I in this?”

“Wait.”

---

Now, ordinarily, a goodbye email followed by a block would end the correspondence.

But this was Ben’s life.

The block did not work.

Discount Marcus shut his book.

“You cannot control the actions of others.”

“I’d settle for controlling the fucking email filter,” I said.

Somewhere, a software engineer had successfully implemented a feature named **Block**, and reality had interpreted it as a suggestion.

The reply arrived.

God put on his reading glasses.

“Does she address Vassil?”

“No.”

“The childhood memories?”

“No.”

“The balcony?”

“No.”

“The award?”

“No.”

“Yavor?”

“No.”

“What does she address?”

I turned the screen around.

> Oh my God so much anger! Just unbelievable! All I can do is just pray for you and hope you can find a good woman one day who can fill out the empty space which your mother left in your life.

There was silence.

Even the air-conditioning stopped, although that may have been unrelated. We buy cheap.

Then I read the last part again.

> …the empty space which your mother left in your life.

I looked at God.

God looked at me.

Discount Marcus looked at the sender.

“Elizabeth,” I said carefully.

“Yes?” said God.

“No. I’m addressing the email.”

“Oh.”

“Elizabeth. **You are the mother.**”

---

I have spent a considerable amount of time around people attempting to distance themselves from unpleasant proceedings.

Usually, they require a lawyer.

Elizabeth had managed it with a noun.

*Your mother.*

As though the woman in question were an unfortunate former contractor whose work she had recently been asked to inspect.

Terrible business, really. Whoever handled the mothering seems to have left a vacancy. Hopefully a good woman can come in and sort that out.

“Should we contact the original provider?” asked Discount Marcus.

“We fucking have.”

“And?”

“She recommends a replacement.”

God rubbed his forehead.

I was beginning to enjoy myself.

---

Then there was the timing of this mysterious empty space.

Ben’s point was that it had been there for years.

Long before the goodbye. Long before the block. Long before this particular exchange.

Yet here it was being discussed as though he had recently removed a load-bearing mother and was now standing in the resulting hole, surprised by the draft.

“Did the space begin when he stopped speaking to her?” God asked.

“Not according to the person who fucking lives there.”

Discount Marcus nodded gravely.

“A man must examine the emptiness within.”

“Marcus, the tenant has submitted a detailed report. Please stop trying to sell him an inspection.”

---

And then:

*All I can do is just pray for you.*

I glanced upstairs.

“Father. Have you taken on a new client?”

“I don’t discuss individual accounts.”

“Ben would like to know when she started fucking praying.”

God checked something behind his desk.

“I’m not getting involved.”

“You’re already mentioned twice. Once by each party. Congratulations, you’re copied in too.”

Discount Marcus tried to look spiritually useful.

“Prayer can bring comfort.”

“Yes, but we are currently trying to determine when this service was activated.”

There was no answer to the memories. No engagement with the specific things Ben had written. There was astonishment at the anger, an appeal to God, and a recruitment opportunity for a future girlfriend.

I pictured the job listing.

*Good woman required. Must be willing to address longstanding vacancy identified by the person previously responsible for the position.*

“Benefits?” asked God.

“Unclear.”

“Salary?”

“Exposure to the family.”

“Absolutely fucking not.”

---

At this point, Ben had options.

He could write another email.

He could revisit every example, establish a numbered chronology, and explain why the response had addressed none of it.

He could ask whether “your mother” was available to join the conversation.

Discount Marcus drew breath.

“He could choose—”

Ben replied.

> GO FUCK YOURSELF!

Marcus closed his mouth.

I leaned back in my chair.

“Well. That should render correctly on mobile.”

God studied the screenshot: her paragraph above, his response below.

“Much shorter than the first email.”

“He’s completed the supporting documentation.”

“No clarification?”

“I believe the requested action is sufficiently clear.”

“No closing salutation?”

“That *is* the closing salutation.”

---

And then Ben laughed.

That was the part I particularly appreciated.

He was laughing at the fucking exchange.

At the enormous email full of things he remembered, followed by a reply that stepped over every one of them, expressed amazement at the anger, and referred him to God and an as-yet-unidentified woman.

At his mother writing about *his mother*.

At the sudden prayer department.

At his own final contribution, sitting underneath the whole thing in magnificent capital letters.

The screenshot required very little editing.

Discount Marcus opened his book again.

“Perhaps this experience will teach—”

“Don’t.”

“A lesson in—”

“Marcus.”

“The importance of—”

I took the book off him.

For once, we had a family email that came with its own fucking punchline. We were going to leave it alone.

---

There remained one practical matter.

The block.

Again.

This time, the specific email address.

God watched Ben deal with it.

“Will it work?”

“I sincerely hope so.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

I looked at the screenshot one more time.

“Then apparently we’ll have to pray.”

Discount Marcus nodded.

Finally, a task within his budget.
