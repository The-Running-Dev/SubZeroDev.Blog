# Binary People Meet a Non-Binary Life

Before somebody misunderstands the title:

This is not about gender.

It is about the human obsession with binary fucking answers.

Yes or no.

This or that.

From here or from there.

Programmer or not programmer.

Bulgarian or American.

Successful or failed.

Pick one.

Apparently ambiguity makes people itchy.

<!-- truncate -->

My life does not cooperate.

## Where Are You From?

Complicated.

## What Do You Do?

Also complicated.

## What Are You?

This interview is deteriorating rapidly.

My identity is fluid.

My occupation is fluid.

Not because I wake up every morning and choose a new personality from a dropdown.

Because life happened.

I lived in different places.

I absorbed different cultures.

I worked in different roles.

I built different things.

I changed.

Then I changed again.

Apparently this is controversial.

## People Want the Checkbox

I understand why categories exist.

Categories are useful.

Databases would be unpleasant without them.

Forms would become essays.

Airport security would collapse entirely.

The problem is when people confuse a useful category with complete reality.

If the form asks for nationality, I can answer the form.

If a government asks for citizenship, there is a legal answer.

That is different from:

> "Who are you?"

Humans are not normalized relational schemas.

At least most of us are not.

Some people on LinkedIn may be.

## Certainty Feels Good

My guess is that certainty is comforting.

If I can classify you, I can stop thinking about you.

I know the box.

The box comes with assumptions.

I can predict things.

Then somebody shows up and says:

> "Eh. Sort of."

Terrible.

Now the model has an unresolved value.

Some people tolerate that easily.

Others start trying to force the value into the nearest available enum.

Bulgarian.

American.

Foreign.

Local.

Technical.

Business.

Whatever.

I keep returning **null** and apparently this is emotionally destabilizing.

## I Am Not Certain Either

This may be the bigger clash.

I am comfortable saying:

> "I don't know."

Or:

> "It depends."

Or:

> "That changed."

Or:

> "I don't care enough to classify it."

I do not need every part of myself to resolve into a final stable definition.

That does not mean I have no sense of self.

It means I do not experience identity as a static database record.

My own description can change when the context changes.

So can my work.

So can my interests.

So can what matters to me.

This seems normal.

Apparently not universally.

## The Occupational Version

The same thing happens with work.

Am I a programmer?

Yes.

No.

Sort of.

I write code.

I architect systems.

I design things.

I build infrastructure.

I automate workflows.

I work on products.

I sometimes spend more time directing AI systems than typing code myself.

At various points I have been a developer, architect, lead, operator, business owner, whatever.

Pick the label that helps the current conversation.

None of them owns me.

That is the occupational version of the nationality argument.

Somebody wants one noun.

I have verbs.

## Maybe the Problem Is Resolution

People keep trying to resolve me.

I keep failing to resolve.

Not because I am deliberately mysterious.

Not because I am hiding something.

Because the truthful answer is often not binary.

It is contextual.

It is historical.

It is messy.

And I do not see any reason to clean it up just so somebody I met five minutes ago can feel administratively satisfied.

Classify me however you like.

I genuinely mean that.

Just do not become upset when I decline to participate in the classification.

Your box is allowed to exist.

I am simply not required to live in it.
