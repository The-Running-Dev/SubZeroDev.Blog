Sometimes a man has got to take a stand.

He has to look at the world, examine his principles, and decide what kind of person he intends to be.

Then Anthropic sends him an email about Opus 5.5 and the entire exercise becomes considerably less philosophical.

**PUT IT ON EVERYTHING.**

And that man is Uncle Ben.

<!-- truncate -->

## The Announcement Was Already Enough

The announcement I received said Opus 5.5 was now the default in Claude Code on my Max plan.

Fine.

I am an adult. I can receive information about a new model without immediately reorganizing my relationship with electricity.

Then I kept reading.

About 25% more mileage from my usage limits compared with Opus 5.

Five-hour limits increased by 20% across models.

Weekly limits unchanged.

That last detail matters. My brain had already started converting percentages into an unlimited supply of bad ideas, which is not how percentages work.

Still.

More room to work.

More room to experiment.

More room to ask an agent to fix one thing and discover that the one thing has seventeen relatives.

This was already a perfectly respectable invitation to spend an evening making GitHub nervous.

Then came the reset.

## Refill the Tanks

One reset for both the five-hour and weekly limits, available to use before October 22 at 9 a.m. Pacific, according to the message.

I appreciate the restraint of the interface.

Somebody presumably wrote a ticket that said:

> Add a clear, accessible way for the customer to reset their usage limits.

What arrived in my head was:

> Would sir like a second afternoon?

Yes.

Sir would.

Sir has several repositories and a deeply unhelpful relationship with the phrase "while we're here."

I could already picture the responsible plan. Pick a useful task. Define the scope. Use the extra capacity deliberately. Review the result.

Then another offer appeared.

## Two Hundred and Fifty Dollars

The cloud-session offer on my screen was for **$250 in credits**.

For cloud sessions specifically. Not a bag of money I could pour into every Claude window I owned.

The screen said the credit had to be claimed by October 8 at 9:59 a.m. GMT+3, and that claimed credit would expire on November 5 at 9:59 a.m. GMT+2. Those were the terms shown to me, not a promise that somebody reading this later can still claim it.

I looked at the amount.

Then the expiry date.

Then, mentally, at every repository I had ever described as "basically there."

This is a dangerous combination.

Giving me temporary compute credit is like giving a man with a garage full of dismantled motorcycles a voucher for more motorcycles.

There will be activity.

Whether the garage becomes easier to walk through is a separate question.

I have very little concern about my ability to turn $250 of available agent compute into $0 of available agent compute.

The challenge is getting some finished work out of the transaction.

## Unfortunately, I Already Had a Plan

I had already been using Codex in the cloud.

I had also started the environment configuration work: the boring, useful machinery that should let a remote agent enter a repository and have the right tools, dependencies, and instructions waiting for it.

**I still need to finish that setup.**

This is an important sentence because there is a version of this story where I announce a beautiful, reproducible, multi-provider engineering operation.

That version would be fiction with a YAML file attached.

The actual plan is straightforward enough:

Prepare the repository. Make the environment repeatable. Give the agent a bounded task. Get back a change and evidence that it works. Review the pull request.

Once the setup is finished and tested, I want to be able to use that pattern with more than one cloud service.

Which means the sensible next step is to finish the environment configuration.

Naturally, the announcement arrived precisely when I was capable of interpreting it as permission to start six other things.

## Capacity Is Not Supervision

The attractive part is obvious.

An agent can work somewhere other than the machine I am currently sitting in front of. I can spend my attention on a different piece of the problem. Work can come back as something I can inspect.

The less attractive part is also obvious, if I stop grinning at the credits long enough to acknowledge it.

I still have to decide what the task is.

I still have to explain what counts as done.

I still have to read the fucking diff.

A larger allowance does not grant a repository immunity from nonsense. It grants me the opportunity to produce more nonsense before the system asks me to take a break.

So the stand has conditions.

Useful tasks. Separate branches. Checks that mean something. Review before anything lands.

Yes, this is disappointingly close to ordinary engineering.

Apparently even my irresponsible enthusiasm needs acceptance criteria.

## The Position Is Clear

Opus 5.5.

Expanded limits.

A reset waiting in reserve.

A cloud-credit offer with an expiry date.

And an unfinished environment setup sitting there like the one adult in the room.

I know what I should do next.

Finish the setup. Prove it works. Give the computers something useful to do.

Then find out how much engineering fits inside that offer before November arrives.

Sometimes a man has got to take a stand.

And sometimes that stand is in front of a usage dashboard, saying:

**"Right. Which repository needs adult supervision first?"**

And that man is Uncle Ben.
