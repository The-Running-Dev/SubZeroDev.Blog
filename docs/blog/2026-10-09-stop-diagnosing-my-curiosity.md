I've heard a lot of suggestions about what might explain the way I think.

ADHD. OCD. Neurodivergence. Some other category somebody recently discovered.

For a while, I entertained some of those possibilities myself.

These days, my response is considerably simpler:

**Eat me.**

<!-- truncate -->

## I Know What My Thinking Feels Like

I wander down rabbit holes.

I associate ideas across programming, history, philosophy, religion, science fiction, and random things I found on the internet fifteen years ago.

I use conversations to examine my own reasoning, not necessarily to argue with anybody.

I know I have biases and blind spots. I deliberately look for them.

Sometimes one thought triggers a connection with another that was already sitting somewhere in my head. Then I follow that. Sometimes I end up back where I began, but with a different understanding of what I was looking at.

I enjoy it.

Very fucking much.

## Someone Always Wants a Label

Someone sees a behavior and suggests a category.

Maybe the behavior is associated with a condition. Maybe it isn't. An observation and a diagnosis aren't the same thing.

But even when a label is accurate, it doesn't automatically tell somebody what a particular experience means to the person having it.

A label can be useful. Plenty of people find one clarifying, relieving, or practically helpful.

I am not disputing that.

I am saying **I don't particularly need someone else's label to explain my curiosity to me.**

## The Pointer Method

I may come across a technical possibility without memorizing its implementation.

Years later, I need something related and think, "Wait, I've seen this somewhere."

I follow the pointer.

I do the same thing with stories, philosophies, and conversations. Information connects with information. Sometimes the connection arrives long after the pieces were acquired.

It reminds me of debugging, rubber ducking, or sleeping on a problem and waking up with a connection that hadn't been conscious the night before.

I don't experience that as some extraordinary revelation.

Often it feels as if the pieces were there the whole time.

## Explaining Isn't the Same as Understanding

People like explanations. So do I, when they actually explain something.

But assigning a category to a behavior can become a substitute for examining the behavior itself.

If you tell me I might have ADHD because I can spend hours exploring a subject, I still want to know what that contributes to our understanding of the particular thinking process we're discussing.

If the answer is "it sounds like ADHD," we've accomplished a naming exercise.

Maybe that name matters in another context. Here, it doesn't add much for me.

## No Diagnosis Required

I don't know everything about how my brain works. That's why I'm curious enough to keep exploring.

I'm also not making a medical claim about what conditions I do or don't have.

I'm describing my experience: knowledge as pointers, conversations as a way to inspect thinking, and a fondness for discovering relationships between things that might have sat separately in memory for years.

You can call it metacognition. Associative thinking. Rubber ducking. Recursive inquiry.

Those terms might be useful descriptions.

None is required for me to enjoy the process.

And if you'd rather insist on diagnosing every rabbit hole than follow it with me?

Well.

**Get over yourself.**
