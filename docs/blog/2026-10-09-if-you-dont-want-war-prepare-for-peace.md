*Si vis pacem, para bellum.* If you want peace, prepare for war.

You've heard that one. I've heard that one. People have been repeating variations of it for centuries.

Then Professor Poutin—fucking Canadians, eh—offered a reversal that stuck with me:

> If you don't want war, prepare for peace.

My version? **If you don't want a fucking war, prepare for fucking peace.**

<!-- truncate -->

## Winning Isn't the Same as Being Done

The professor's examples included the aftermath of World War I: Germany's defeat, the reparations and political upheaval that followed, and the resentment that became part of the environment preceding another catastrophe.

There were many causes of World War II. That's not a claim that reparations alone caused it.

What interested me was the underlying question: **what happens to people after you've beaten their country?**

You can defeat an army and still leave behind a population with shattered livelihoods, destroyed infrastructure, humiliation, and reasons to resent you.

What have you prepared for then?

A peace treaty is a document. A durable peace is an entirely different project.

## I've Seen the Resentment

I remember driving through Yugoslavia carrying an American passport and encountering hostility because of it.

It wasn't a theoretical lecture. People saw the passport and had opinions about the United States and what had happened to their country.

I wasn't personally responsible for American military policy. That distinction didn't make their reactions disappear.

And that experience stayed with me.

You can explain geopolitical intervention in strategic terms, talk about military objectives, draw arrows on maps, and debate the justifications for a campaign. Then you meet someone who lived through the consequences. Their memory doesn't end where the military operation ended.

## The Question Nobody Wants to Stay With

The same concern came to mind while considering Iraq, Afghanistan, and the Israel–Palestine conflict.

Not that the wars were identical. Not that every grievance is legitimate. Not that every violent response is excusable.

Rather: **how do you build conditions that make another generation less likely to want another war?**

I once tried exploring the history of Hamas and Israel with a woman living in Israel. I didn't have a settled position at that time; I wanted to understand how she saw it.

The discussion became emotionally charged. Asking about what one side had done was heard as excusing what the other side had done.

But understanding a cause isn't the same as endorsing an act. I can condemn terrorism and still ask what produced the conditions in which violence continues.

If the only acceptable response is to point at the other side, we never reach the question of what happens afterward.

## The Roman Problem

"Prepare for war" makes immediate intuitive sense when you're worried about being attacked.

But the professor's inversion shifts the question from deterring a fight to preventing the next one.

Peace requires more than the absence of an enemy army. It requires attention to the people and conditions left behind.

And yes, sometimes the world requires defense and force. That isn't what I'm debating here.

I'm interested in the part that comes afterward—and often gets neglected until the next conflict begins.

**If you don't want a fucking war, prepare for fucking peace.**

Not a slogan to end every argument. A question worth taking seriously.
