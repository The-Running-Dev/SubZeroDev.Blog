# You Don't Get Time. You Make It.

Someone told me:

> “I'll do it when I get some time.”

My brain immediately objected.

<!-- truncate -->

Get time?

From where?

Who distributes it?

Is there a warehouse?

Can I buy some wholesale?

So I replied:

> “You can purchase some at Metro at a discount.”

Perfectly reasonable.

If time is something we **get**, there must be a supply chain.

## The English Is Fine

Yes.

I know people say:

> “When I get some time.”

It is idiomatic English.

Nobody needs to write me a grammar lesson.

The phrase works.

That is not why it bothered me.

The first joke was simply linguistic.

Take “get” literally.

Now time becomes a commodity.

Done.

But then my brain kept going.

Because language sometimes reveals a frame.

## Get Time

“When I get some time.”

Time arrives.

Time becomes available.

Time is granted.

Time is acquired.

The person waits.

Maybe circumstances eventually release a small shipment.

This framing quietly places agency somewhere else.

That amused me.

Because I do not think of time that way.

## Have Time

Then there is:

> “When I have time.”

Better.

Less transactional.

But still passive.

You either have it or you do not.

Time is apparently an inventory state.

Check stock.

Out of stock.

Try again later.

## Make Time

The phrase I actually like is:

> “I'll make time.”

Now something changed.

The calendar is no longer weather.

Time is not arriving.

Time is not being issued.

You are deciding that one thing matters enough to displace another thing.

That is agency.

## No, This Is Not Absolute

Obviously people have constraints.

Jobs.

Children.

Care responsibilities.

Deadlines.

Illness.

Money.

Travel.

Bureaucracy.

Life.

You cannot simply manifest arbitrary free hours by believing in yourself hard enough.

That is not the point.

The point is that inside the constraints, choices still exist.

When somebody says:

> “I don't have time.”

sometimes the more precise sentence is:

> “This is not high enough on my priority list to displace something else.”

That sounds harsher.

It is also often more honest.

## The Universe Is Apparently Involved

The same conversation later became:

> “The Universe doesn't like me lately.”

Why?

Because she had to work.

Again, I became fascinated by the agency leak.

Work was something imposed by cosmic hostility.

Time was something to be acquired from somewhere.

Eventually I wanted to ask whether the Universe loved me because I did not need to work.

If employment is proof of divine disfavor, apparently my schedule is evidence of celestial affection.

This is why nobody should let me near metaphors unsupervised.

## The Joke Was Better Than the Philosophy

I did not start by thinking:

> “I shall now interrogate the metaphysics of time allocation.”

I saw “get some time” and imagined a retail transaction.

That was the joke.

The philosophy arrived later.

That is usually how these things happen.

First:

> “Can I get some time at Metro?”

Then, twenty minutes later:

> “Actually, what does that phrase imply about agency?”

This is why simple conversations become expensive.

## Make the Time

I am not prescribing a productivity system.

I am not saying everybody should work harder.

I am not selling a calendar method.

I am saying the language is interesting.

Get time.

Have time.

Make time.

Three constructions.

Three slightly different relationships to the same finite resource.

And if somebody tells me they will do something when they **get** some time, I may still ask where they plan to purchase it.

Because apparently Metro has everything.
