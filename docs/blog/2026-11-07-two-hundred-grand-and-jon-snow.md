# Two Hundred Grand and Jon Snow

There is something magnificent about being lectured on money by a Tinder biography.

<!-- truncate -->

You open the app. You don't even match with the person. You just see a profile declaring that whoever said money can't solve your problems must not have had enough money to solve them.

Well.

There's a theory.

The woman was eighteen, according to what I saw. I'm fifty. And after half a century on this ridiculous planet, I've accumulated a few data points about money.

I've worked three jobs at one point in my life. I've made two hundred thousand dollars in a year. I've had plenty of money. I've had less. I've watched it come and go. I've had problems throughout.

Money paid bills. Absolutely. Money bought time, choices, equipment, freedom to move, a little breathing room. I'm not here to pretend a full bank account doesn't do anything.

But I've never found the secret account balance that suddenly makes a human life immune to suffering.

Two hundred grand in a year didn't come with a certificate saying I was finished having problems.

And I didn't have to be poor to find that out.

That's why the quote irritated me. Not because she was eighteen. Eighteen-year-olds are allowed to have opinions. God knows I had plenty. It's the certainty of the thing.

If money doesn't fix your problem, you simply didn't have enough.

What a convenient fucking argument. Whatever happens, the theory survives. Get more money. Still have problems? Ah, you needed even more.

How much? At what point do you reach enlightenment? Is there an express lane for millionaires? Does emotional equilibrium unlock when the account crosses seven figures?

I had half a lecture ready in my head.

Then I looked at what I was doing.

Why am I preparing to explain fifty years of living to a complete stranger on a dating app?

She hadn't asked for my résumé. We hadn't matched. She knew nothing about me, and I knew nothing about the actual state of her finances. The profile struck me as gold-digging advertising, but that's my reading of the presentation, not a financial audit.

I don't have to prove my net worth just to disagree with a sentence.

So I sent her the much shorter answer:

**“Oh John Snow, you know nothing.”**

Yes, *John* as I typed it, not the television character's *Jon*. Apparently my contempt for this theory also extended to proofreading.

A tiny joke, in place of a spectacularly unnecessary argument.

And I meant it.

I've earned money. I've spent money. I've helped people. I've been helped less often than I would have liked. I've been around long enough to know that wealth and wisdom are not synonyms.

If money is everything to you, fine. Go make some. Build something. Work for it. Get as much as you want.

Just don't tell me that anyone whose life still has problems must be financially incompetent.

I've been the guy with the paycheck.

The problems didn't politely leave when it cleared.

And that, rather than an elaborate dating-app sermon, was the whole point of my six-word response.

She didn't have to understand it.

I was amused enough for both of us.
