# The Miracle of a Good Word

I admire my mother's independence.

That belongs in the story.

It would be easier to leave it out and produce a straightforward account of everything I didn't get from her. But I recognized something in her that deserved recognition, and I told her.

A good word. Apparently a more complicated family technology than it first appears.

<!-- truncate -->

I grew up with criticism where I wanted encouragement.

There is a particular poverty in that. Somebody can keep finding things to say about you while leaving you hungry for a single sentence you can actually live on.

You learn that your deficiencies are available for discussion.

Your worth is apparently awaiting further review.

I wanted encouragement. Affection. Praise. The ordinary recognition that a person might hope to receive without first becoming the central figure in a painful confrontation.

Yet the good words seemed to emerge after those confrontations.

*Seemed* matters. I am describing how the pattern felt to me. I am not claiming to have an inventory of every kind thing my mother ever said, or a secret explanation for why she said it.

But when affection and praise appear to follow pain, the experience is fucking bewildering.

There you are, finally receiving something you wanted, and part of you is still dealing with what happened before it.

Should you be pleased?

Of course the words matter. That is why the shortage mattered in the first place.

But why did the route to them have to hurt?

I could admire my mother's independence without pretending that independence had supplied everything I needed from her. Recognizing a strength in somebody is not a signed declaration that the relationship contains no injuries.

She could be independent.

I could admire it.

I could still have needed more kindness.

All of those things fit inside one family, however inconvenient they are for a simple verdict.

I recognize versions of these generational patterns in Bulgaria too: criticism that comes readily, encouragement that seems harder to release. I am talking about patterns I notice, not announcing the emotional constitution of an entire country. People are people. They do not all parent from the same fucking manual.

Still, when something feels familiar across generations, I wonder what gets passed on without anyone quite deciding to pass it on.

What does a person learn when correction is plentiful and praise is scarce?

What do they then consider normal to give?

That can help me ask a better question. It does not answer every question about my mother, and it does not make my own childhood needs evaporate.

Understanding a pattern is not the same as being fed by it.

And perhaps that is why the good word can feel so miraculous.

It is such a small thing to offer. Notice something admirable. Say it. Let the other person hear that you see more than what needs fixing.

No elaborate ceremony required.

I was able to look at my mother and recognize her independence. Whatever else was happening between us, I could say that something about her was worthy of admiration.

I wanted some version of that ease coming back toward me.

Not praise manufactured to settle an argument. Not affection I felt I had to reach through a painful confrontation.

Just a good word, given freely.

It shouldn't need to be a fucking miracle.
