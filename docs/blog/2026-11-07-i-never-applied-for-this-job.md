# I Never Applied for This Job

Apparently I became the permanent caregiver for our mother somewhere between doing things that needed doing and nobody else asking whether I intended to keep doing them forever. I would appreciate a copy of the fucking appointment letter.

<!-- truncate -->

Elizabeth has three sons. I am the oldest.

That explains the order in which we were born. It does not, by itself, explain who agreed to organize the rest of his life around her.

I helped. For years.

There was driving, arranging things, preparing places to live, repairs, furniture, money, errands, and the work that only becomes visible when somebody stops doing it. Martin and I helped her through nursing school by typing her long medical papers. Friends and family helped with work on her properties too.

I did those things because she was my mother and because I believed helping was the thing to do.

Some arrangements benefited me as well. I have said that plainly. Living somewhere without paying rent benefited me. Preparing and improving the place benefited her. Mutual benefit did not make my contribution imaginary.

Spain was supposed to be a workable arrangement. We had discussed her providing the purchase money upfront, with me working, contributing, and paying at least half back.

We looked at around thirty houses. The lease was running out. Decisions changed. Keeping the agreed plan moving became, in her account, pressure.

Eventually I said I could not keep living together under those conditions. She agreed to find a room in Spain. I went to Bulgaria for two weeks.

I returned still considering alternatives. Separate apartments, perhaps, close enough for me to help without sharing the same home.

When I got back, she was gone. There was a letter.

She and my younger brother had arranged for her to go to America and live with him. Neither had called me before making that arrangement.

Then his email arrived describing how he had been forced to restructure the next ten to fifteen years of his life, while I had apparently left town and carried on with mine.

Hang on a fucking second.

At what point did the three of us discuss making me her permanent caregiver?

At what point did I agree that stepping back from one living arrangement authorized somebody to appoint me responsible for the next one?

I did not invite her into his house. He did.

He might have felt an obligation to help her. I understand that feeling. It has occupied a considerable amount of my life too. But feeling an obligation does not mean somebody else made your decision.

His email wished for a smoother transition of care. I kept stopping at that phrase.

Transition from what agreed role?

We had discussed financial contributions when substantial care became necessary: nurses, a care home, something comparable. That was a specific discussion. It was not an agreement that I would remain the primary caregiver indefinitely, or fund whatever living arrangement somebody else established.

I had a life. He had a life. So did Martin.

When I was handling the practical burden, I needed support too. Often I wanted a phone call. When the arrangement became difficult for him, the consequences to his time and future became central to his judgment of my character.

I wanted the same recognition for the time I had already given.

And then there was the family judgment I had lived with: not enough, not successful enough, not doing enough. Somehow the person who had fallen short could simultaneously be the person expected to carry this enormous responsibility.

Whatever anybody thought of my achievements, it did not establish my agreement to that assignment.

I can be responsible for what I actually promised. I can answer for what I did. I can acknowledge mistakes in how I handled something difficult.

I am not accepting that years of helping quietly became an unlimited commitment, or that another adult's invitation became my decision because he regretted its consequences.

I did the work I did. I also had limits.

There were three sons.

I keep wondering when that stopped meaning there were three adults whose choices counted.
