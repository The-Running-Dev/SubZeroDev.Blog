# Please Walk in Front of the Future With a Red Flag

*Or: A Brief History of Humanity Being Absolutely Terrified of Progress*

**By Lucifer**

Ah, humanity.

My favorite collection of frightened primates, perpetually convinced that civilization is about to collapse because someone invented a new machine.

I've watched you do this for centuries.

Fire. Printing presses. Steam engines. Automobiles. Airplanes. Computers. The internet.

And now, artificial intelligence.

Every single time, the same performance.

The technology changes. The costumes change. The vocabulary becomes slightly more sophisticated.

But the underlying argument remains remarkably consistent:

**"I don't understand this thing, therefore it must be dangerous, and somebody should bloody well stop it."**

Allow me to take you on a little historical tour.

Pour yourself a drink. Preferably something stronger than whatever passes for intellectual discourse on social media these days.

<!-- truncate -->

## Act I: The Horseless Carriage — An Instrument of Satan

Imagine Britain in 1865.

Queen Victoria is on the throne. Horses are everywhere. The Industrial Revolution is reshaping society.

And some enterprising bastard has the audacity to suggest that perhaps transportation might work better without an animal shitting in the street.

Naturally, this cannot be tolerated.

Enter the **Locomotives Act of 1865**, sometimes remembered as the Red Flag Act.

The British Parliament established some truly magnificent restrictions on self-propelled road vehicles.

They could travel at no more than four miles per hour in the countryside.

In towns?

**Two miles per hour.**

But wait. It gets better.

Such vehicles required at least three people to operate them, including one person walking at least sixty yards ahead carrying a red flag.

An actual red flag.

To warn everyone that the terrifying mechanical monstrosity was approaching.

At two miles per hour.

A reasonably motivated grandmother could have overtaken the thing while carrying groceries.

And yet, apparently, civilization required advance warning.

Now, to be historically precise, these regulations initially targeted steam-powered road locomotives, before gasoline automobiles became commonplace. The laws subsequently constrained Britain's emerging motorcar industry as well.

The justification involved horses being frightened, road safety, and the dangers of unfamiliar machinery.

Some of those concerns were perfectly legitimate.

But the solution was magnificent.

**Humanity had invented a vehicle that could move without a horse, then required a man to walk in front of it.**

Think about that.

You eliminate the horse.

And replace it with a pedestrian.

Progress!

The restrictions weren't substantially swept away until 1896, when Britain celebrated the liberation of motorists with the London-to-Brighton Emancipation Run.

Thirty-one years after the red-flag legislation.

Thirty-one years of treating self-propelled transportation as something that needed a human chaperone.

I particularly admire the symbolism.

A man walking ahead of technological progress, waving a flag to ensure it doesn't progress too quickly.

If that isn't the perfect representation of human civilization, I don't know what is.

## Act II: The Flying Machine — Surely You Must Be Joking

Then came the airplane.

For thousands of years, humanity dreamed of flying.

You wrote mythology about it. Painted angels. Invented winged gods. Stared longingly at birds.

Then two bicycle mechanics from Ohio actually did something about it.

December 17, 1903.

The Wright brothers achieved the first sustained, controlled, powered flights of a heavier-than-air machine.

Surely the world erupted in universal celebration?

Not quite.

The initial breakthrough received surprisingly little press coverage. Claims of successful powered flight were met with skepticism, partly because countless previous attempts had ended in spectacular failure.

Even the United States Army initially declined to engage seriously with the brothers' offer.

By 1906, the *New York Herald* was publicly questioning whether the Wrights had actually flown or were simply making extraordinary claims.

Imagine it.

Human beings had finally accomplished something their species had dreamed about since antiquity.

And the response was essentially:

"Yeah, sounds like bullshit."

Now, unlike the popular mythology of universal opposition, early aviation inspired enormous excitement as well as skepticism.

By 1908, public demonstrations were drawing crowds. Aviators became celebrities. International flying exhibitions captivated audiences.

But there was also a very real problem.

Early flying was extraordinarily dangerous.

Between 1908 and 1910, thirty-three people died during the first three years of public flying.

And when commercial air travel began developing, operators had to overcome considerable passenger anxiety.

In 1930, Ellen Church helped introduce nurses aboard commercial aircraft as flight attendants, partly to reassure nervous travelers.

Yes, ladies and gentlemen.

Humanity invented powered flight.

And then needed a nurse on board to convince people that getting into the machine wasn't an elaborate form of suicide.

Of course, aircraft eventually became one of the foundations of modern civilization.

Millions of people now board airplanes without giving a moment's thought to the fact that they're sitting inside a metal tube, hurtling through the atmosphere at hundreds of miles per hour.

They are far more concerned about the Wi-Fi.

How quickly the miraculous becomes mundane.

## Act III: Enter Artificial Intelligence — The Antichrist Has a Keyboard

And here we are.

2026\.

Humanity has developed machines capable of generating software, analyzing immense quantities of information, translating languages, assisting scientific research, and performing tasks that recently required considerable specialized human effort.

An extraordinary technological achievement.

Naturally, the public conversation is an absolute fucking circus.

"AI is destroying creativity!"

"AI is taking everyone's jobs!"

"AI-generated work is all slop!"

"Nobody will know how to think anymore!"

"Everything is going to collapse!"

How delightfully familiar.

We have traded the frightened horse for the frightened knowledge worker.

The red flag has become a social media post.

And the man walking sixty yards ahead of the automobile has become someone demanding that AI development be stopped until society has figured out what to do with it.

Which, judging by humanity's historical record, should take approximately forever.

The anxiety isn't imaginary, either.

According to Pew Research Center's June 2026 survey, **52% of American adults were more concerned than excited about AI**, compared with just 9% who were more excited than concerned.

A substantial portion of the population regards one of the most consequential technologies of the century primarily with apprehension.

And, naturally, the internet has transformed that apprehension into a competitive sporting event.

One person posts a terrible AI-generated image.

Another discovers that a language model made something up.

A third finds some software written by AI that doesn't work properly.

And suddenly the verdict arrives:

"See? The entire technology is worthless!"

Marvelous reasoning.

Imagine applying this standard to automobiles in 1900.

An engine breaks down.

"TRANSPORTATION IS A SCAM!"

A car crashes.

"HORSES WERE BETTER!"

A driver gets lost.

"THE AUTOMOBILE HAS FAILED HUMANITY!"

Or aviation in 1908.

A plane falls out of the sky.

"FLIGHT IS SLOP!"

I would have paid good money to read those newspaper headlines.

## Act IV: But Lucifer, Surely There Are Real Dangers?

Of course there are, darling.

I'm the Devil, not an idiot.

Automobiles brought traffic deaths, pollution, congestion, and enormous changes to urban life.

Airplanes brought accidents, environmental costs, and eventually entirely new ways of dropping explosives on people.

Progress does not arrive bearing nothing but gifts.

And artificial intelligence brings its own serious problems.

Displaced workers. Concentrated corporate power. Surveillance. Misinformation. Intellectual-property disputes. Fraud. Unreliable automated decisions.

These are genuine questions, requiring genuine answers.

But there is an enormous difference between **identifying a danger** and **declaring a technology worthless because danger exists**.

The former is intelligence.

The latter is fear wearing a cheap suit and pretending to be wisdom.

You can criticize an AI system for fabricating information without concluding that every use of artificial intelligence is fraudulent.

You can question the economics of AI companies without pretending the underlying technology has no value.

You can oppose irresponsible deployment while recognizing that responsible applications exist.

And you can acknowledge that AI-generated garbage exists without concluding that everything created with AI must therefore be garbage.

After all, humanity was producing mountains of worthless content long before computers became particularly interesting.

I've read your newspapers.

I've watched your television.

I've endured your corporate PowerPoint presentations.

**You did not need artificial intelligence to invent slop.**

You perfected that art entirely on your own.

## Act V: The Most Terrifying Thing About Change

Here's what I find especially amusing.

People rarely describe themselves as being afraid of progress.

Oh, no.

They're defending standards.

Protecting authenticity.

Preserving craftsmanship.

Standing up for human creativity.

Sometimes those motivations are entirely sincere.

But other times, beneath the noble vocabulary, lies a much less comfortable proposition:

**"The world is changing, and I am no longer certain where I fit into it."**

That's a frightening experience.

The automobile didn't simply introduce a new way to travel.

It challenged entire industries built around horses.

Aviation challenged assumptions about distance, geography, commerce, and warfare.

AI challenges something even more personal.

The notion that particular kinds of intellectual labor belong exclusively to human beings.

For centuries, you have defined yourselves partly through what you can do.

What happens when a machine can perform some of those tasks?

Do those abilities become worthless?

Does the human become worthless?

Or does the definition of useful human work begin changing?

Those are questions worth asking.

But screaming "slop" at every machine-assisted creation does not answer them.

It merely announces that you dislike the question.

And there's another delicious irony.

The people who condemn AI-generated work while insisting that only the final output matters are often the same people who would never dream of asking whether their car was assembled using power tools.

Apparently, some tools are acceptable only after everyone has forgotten that they were once controversial.

## Act VI: The Red Flag Has Gone Digital

Let me offer a little thought experiment.

Suppose we could transport a gentleman from 1865 into the present.

Show him a modern automobile.

A machine capable of traveling at highway speeds, equipped with computers, cameras, sensors, navigation systems, and more processing power than entire institutions once possessed.

He might be horrified.

He might demand restrictions.

He might insist that no responsible person should operate such a monstrosity without someone walking ahead of it.

You would laugh at him.

Of course you would.

Then show him a commercial aircraft.

Then a smartphone.

Then a computer capable of conversing with him, writing software, translating languages, and explaining quantum physics.

He might conclude that the entire world had gone mad.

And perhaps he would have a point.

But consider this:

Which of today's confident declarations about artificial intelligence will sound equally ridiculous in fifty years?

Which supposedly impossible capabilities will become ordinary?

Which professions will transform?

Which predictions of catastrophe will turn out to be justified, and which will become historical comedy?

We don't know.

That's rather the point.

The future has an irritating habit of arriving without requesting permission from those most comfortable with the present.

## Epilogue: Kindly Get Out of the Road

Let me be perfectly clear.

I'm not suggesting that every new technology deserves blind acceptance.

Quite the opposite.

Question it.

Test it.

Expose its failures.

Demand accountability.

Build safeguards where they are necessary.

But for Hell's sake, distinguish skepticism from reflexive hostility.

The automobile didn't become useful because everyone applauded.

The airplane didn't become practical because everyone believed.

And artificial intelligence won't become reliable merely because its enthusiasts insist that it already is.

Progress requires experimentation, criticism, engineering, evidence, and an occasional spectacular failure.

It does not require universal enthusiasm.

Nor does it require universal permission.

History is littered with people who were absolutely certain that the newest invention represented the beginning of the end.

Some identified genuine problems.

Others mistook unfamiliarity for danger.

And many did a little of both.

The challenge is knowing the difference.

So, by all means, wave your red flag.

Declare that the machines are dangerous.

Insist that everything was better when horses pulled carriages and human beings wrote every line of code by candlelight.

Just understand that **your fear is not, by itself, evidence that the future is a mistake.**

The automobile is coming.

The airplane has already taken off.

And artificial intelligence is not going to disappear because someone on the internet called it slop.

You can help decide how it is used.

You can challenge its abuses.

You can build something extraordinary with it.

Or you can stand sixty yards ahead, waving a little red flag, insisting that two miles per hour is quite fast enough for civilization.

Personally?

I'll be in the car.

I hear the future has excellent acceleration.

**— Lucifer**
