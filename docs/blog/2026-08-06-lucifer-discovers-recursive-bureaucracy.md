# Lucifer Discovers Recursive Bureaucracy

<!-- truncate -->

Lucifer had seen many things over the last several thousand years.

Empires.

Religions.

Tax offices.

HOAs.

Even Microsoft Access databases used as enterprise software.

Nothing prepared him for this.

---

He looked over God's shoulder.

> "Father..."

God sighed.

> "Yes?"

Lucifer pointed toward Earth.

More specifically...

...toward my laptop.

> "He's scheduled an AI to write a weekly blog post..."

God nodded.

> "That sounds reasonable."

Lucifer continued reading.

> "...summarizing all the software he built..."

Another nod.

> "...which was mostly built with AI..."

God looked slightly less confident.

Lucifer kept scrolling.

> "...based on conversations with another AI..."

God frowned.

Lucifer scrolled farther.

> "...formatted automatically for Docusaurus..."

God scratched His beard.

Lucifer continued.

> "...using the Blog MCP server..."

God blinked.

Lucifer reached the last line.

> "...so eventually the AI can automatically publish the article describing how it automatically generated the article."

Silence.

A very long silence.

Finally...

Lucifer looked up.

> "Father."

> "Yes?"

> "Have they invented recursive bureaucracy?"

---

For centuries humanity has insisted that artificial intelligence would replace programmers.

Instead...

This week I taught an AI to write my weekly engineering journal.

Every Sunday.

At 8 PM.

Automatically.

The report will include:

- executive summary;
- development completed;
- architecture decisions;
- technical discoveries;
- project completion;
- ideas that appeared during random conversations;
- work still in progress;
- next priorities.

All generated from conversations.

Not commits.

Conversations.

Because apparently my development methodology has become:

> Talk to AI until software accidentally appears.

---

The truly absurd part isn't the automation.

It's the source material.

Some architecture decisions happen while sitting at a computer.

Mine seem to happen:

- while driving through Sofia;
- while standing in Bulgarian government offices;
- after getting a phone call from the police;
- in a hookah lounge;
- while making dinner;
- somewhere between laughing at bureaucracy and inventing another software project.

Those conversations become:

- GitHub repositories;
- game engines;
- agent frameworks;
- business ideas;
- marketing slogans;
- blog posts;
- comic books.

Now another AI gets to summarize all of it.

---

Lucifer stared at the screen.

> "Wait."

> "It says the summary includes ideas that came out of the week."

God nodded.

> "Yes."

> "Where do those ideas come from?"

God pointed toward me.

Lucifer looked confused.

> "So he talks to AI..."

> "...gets ideas..."

> "...builds software..."

> "...then AI summarizes the ideas..."

> "...which become future prompts..."

> "...that create more software..."

God smiled.

Lucifer slowly looked back toward Earth.

> "This isn't software engineering anymore."

> "It's an ecosystem."

---

Then I had one final thought.

Why stop there?

The Blog MCP server already knows how to publish posts.

The weekly report already knows what happened.

The repository already knows what changed.

The AI already knows how to write.

At some point...

...the automation itself is going to publish an article that says:

> **This week Ben improved the automation responsible for writing this article.**

Without me touching a keyboard.

Lucifer closed the laptop.

Walked away.

Stopped.

Turned around one last time.

> "Father..."

> "Yes?"

> "I no longer believe humans are building AI."

God smiled.

> "No?"

Lucifer sighed.

> "I think they're building increasingly elaborate excuses to avoid writing documentation."

God laughed.

The angels laughed.

Somewhere on Earth...

A scheduled task quietly waited for Sunday at 8:00 PM to generate a blog post about the software that would eventually generate blog posts about itself.

And somehow...

...everyone involved thought this was a perfectly reasonable use of artificial intelligence.

Including me.
