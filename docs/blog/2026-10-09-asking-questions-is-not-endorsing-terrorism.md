I once spent an evening talking to a woman who lived in Israel.

I enjoyed the conversation. That's worth establishing because people have a remarkable talent for rewriting these encounters into battles.

At the time, I didn't know enough about Hamas and Israel to claim a settled opinion. She'd expressed dissatisfaction with things her government was doing, and I thought: here's someone with a firsthand perspective. I can ask questions.

So I did.

<!-- truncate -->

## When Questions Become Allegiances

I asked about the wider history, about things Israel had done, about things Hamas had done, about how people there understood the conflict.

I was trying to understand the landscape, not volunteer for a team.

As the evening progressed, I could see her becoming uncomfortable and then upset. Eventually my questions were interpreted as condoning terrorism.

Well, fuck.

That's an extraordinary conclusion to reach from someone asking you to explain how you see something.

I wasn't defending Hamas. I wasn't defending Israel. I wasn't even attempting to convince her that some conclusion I'd already reached was correct.

I didn't have that conclusion.

If I'd met someone associated with Hamas, I'd have wanted to ask questions about that person's perspective too. That doesn't confer endorsement. It's curiosity.

## Questions Are Not Votes

This is something I keep encountering.

Somebody tells me what they believe. I ask what led them there. I examine an apparent inconsistency or ask about another account of the same event.

Suddenly I'm assumed to support the opposition.

Apparently every question is a declaration of loyalty.

It isn't.

A question might be a request for evidence. It might be an effort to connect two facts. It might expose a weakness in my *own* thinking. Sometimes I ask it because I genuinely don't know.

That's not a clever way of disguising a position. That's how I examine ideas.

## The Familiar Nationality Routine

I've run into a similar pattern in Bulgaria.

People will complain about the government themselves, sometimes at considerable length. Then I criticize something, and suddenly I'm both Bulgarian and not Bulgarian in whichever combination disqualifies me from commenting.

It's fascinating.

The content of the criticism may be the same. The reception changes depending on who says it and what the listener believes that person is allowed to question.

## What I Took From That Evening

I didn't come away with a grand theory about everyone who lives in Israel. I met one person and had one conversation.

I came away thinking about how easily an inquiry into history can become a test of identity.

I understand that these subjects have emotional weight for people. In an actual conversation, I pay attention to that: tone, expression, body language, opportunities to change course.

But I still want to understand things.

**Asking why something happened is not saying it was justified.**

And asking someone what they think is not asking them to join an argument I haven't even decided to have.

Sometimes I just want to talk, learn, and see which dots connect.

Apparently that can be controversial enough on its own.
