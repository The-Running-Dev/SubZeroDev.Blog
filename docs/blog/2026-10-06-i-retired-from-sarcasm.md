# I Retired From Sarcasm

I can be sarcastic.

That is not the problem.

I can be **extremely** sarcastic.

Dry enough that people do not know I am joking.

That used to be a problem.

Especially in the United States.

<!-- truncate -->

I would say something completely ridiculous with a straight face.

Nobody would laugh.

Then I would discover that the other person had filed the statement under:

**THING BEN ACTUALLY BELIEVES.**

Wonderful.

Eventually I stopped.

Not because I lost the ability.

Because I stopped liking the mechanism.

## Sarcasm Is Indirect

The basic structure of sarcasm is strange.

You say one thing.

You mean another.

The listener is expected to detect the mismatch.

Often the real payload is criticism.

So instead of saying:

> “I think that idea is bad.”

you say:

> “Oh yeah, brilliant idea.”

Now everybody gets to participate in a little interpretive ritual.

Did you mean it?

Did you not mean it?

How sharp was it?

Was it affectionate?

Was it contempt?

Was it passive aggression?

Wonderful.

Very efficient.

## “A High Form of Communication”

I have heard sarcasm described as some kind of high form of communication.

That has always amused me.

If it is such an elevated art, why does every second person seem perfectly capable of doing it?

People grow up with it.

Families use it.

Friends use it.

Coworkers use it.

Entire cultures use it as background noise.

That does not make it bad.

It just makes the “high form” claim a little grandiose.

## What I Do Instead

I still mock things.

Constantly.

I still make absurd jokes.

Constantly.

I still take somebody's wording and push it until it becomes ridiculous.

I still say things like:

> “Where do you get time? Is Metro selling it?”

I still hear:

> “I may check on you later.”

and respond:

> “May the checks be with you.”

I still hear:

> “I was working on bugs and the girl went to the gym.”

and ask:

> “Did the bugs go too?”

That is not sarcasm.

It is literalism.

Wordplay.

Premise abuse.

Absurd escalation.

I am not saying the opposite of what I mean.

I am saying exactly what the sentence triggered in my head.

## Directness Is Easier to Debug

If I think something makes no sense, I would rather say:

> “That makes no sense.”

If I think a premise is ridiculous, I would rather attack the premise directly.

If I think somebody is being evasive, I would rather say that.

There is less decoding.

You may still dislike what I said.

Fine.

But at least we are arguing about the thing I actually meant.

## The Old Version

The older version of me could absolutely weaponize sarcasm.

Deadpan.

Flat delivery.

No visible signal.

Sometimes people genuinely could not tell.

At the time I probably thought that was funny.

Sometimes it was.

Sometimes it was just poor interface design.

If the user cannot reliably tell whether the message is literal, maybe the protocol sucks.

## The New Version Is Still Annoying

Dropping sarcasm did not make me normal.

That would have been too easy.

I simply replaced one form of verbal trouble with several others.

Now I am direct.

Literal.

Absurd.

Occasionally theatrical.

Sometimes I cross-examine metaphysical claims like a discount-store television attorney.

Sometimes I turn one badly phrased sentence into ten minutes of entertainment.

So this is not a story about becoming polite.

Absolutely not.

It is a story about removing one layer of indirection.

## Say What You Mean

If I tell you:

> “That makes no sense.”

I mean:

> “That makes no sense to me.”

Not:

> “I secretly hate you.”

Not:

> “You are stupid.”

Not:

> “Women cannot reason.”

Not whatever additional payload somebody may infer.

If there is more, I can say more.

That is the point.

Sarcasm asks the listener to decode hidden meaning.

I would rather put the meaning on the table.

Then, once everybody knows what the hell we are talking about, we can make it absurd.
