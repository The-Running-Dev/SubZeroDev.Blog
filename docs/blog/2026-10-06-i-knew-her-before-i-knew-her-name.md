# I Knew Her Before I Knew Her Name

I talked to a woman on Bumble for about two hours.

Songs.

Voice messages.

Comedy.

Writing.

Dating.

Philosophy.

Personal shit.

Her old music.

My old music.

Somewhere in there she told me I was complicated.

Fair.

Eventually I looked at the conversation and realized something.

I had no idea what her actual name was.

<!-- truncate -->

Her profile said Moonflower.

So I called her Moonflower.

Why would I not?

That was the name in front of me.

Then, after two hours:

> Moonflower, I just realized you never gave me your name.

And the even funnier realization:

**I never asked.**

Because apparently I did not need it.

## Meanwhile, She Wanted My "Real" Name

This became ridiculous because she had spent part of the conversation trying to establish my real name.

My name is Ben.

That is what I call myself.

That is what people around me call me.

Apparently that was insufficient.

What is the birth-certificate name?

Why does that matter?

There is history attached to that name.

Psychology.

Family.

Identity.

Things I did not particularly feel like unpacking for someone I had just met.

But more importantly:

**You already have my name.**

Ben.

Operational.

Tested.

Works in production.

Why do you need the database key?

## Connection Before Biography

This is where the whole conversation became funny to me.

She wanted:

Name.

Origin.

Occupation.

The usual identity coordinates.

And I was perfectly happy with:

Moonflower.

A voice.

A song.

A joke.

A person currently talking to me.

I learned that she used to write and sing.

She sent me one of her own songs from years ago.

I heard her voice.

I told her she had some fucking voice.

She told me her father had wanted her to become a folklore singer.

That told me more about her than a legal name ever could.

## Extraordinary Machine

The best part was the song game.

She sent Fiona Apple's *Extraordinary Machine* as a kind of introduction.

I listened.

Then I sent Lynyrd Skynyrd's *Simple Man*.

Not as a clever response.

It was simply the song that arrived.

She told me she used to sing that one too.

Of course she did.

Then she shared one of her own recordings.

That changed the game.

Now she was not sending me a song she liked.

She was sending me something she had made.

Something personal.

So I sent Joan Baez.

*Diamonds & Rust.*

A song somebody once showed me that stayed with me because it is intimate, painful, beautiful, and full of history.

I am not a singer.

That was my answer.

Different medium.

Same level of disclosure.

## We Learned More Through Songs

This is what amused me afterward.

We had spent part of the conversation trying to answer the standard questions.

Where are you from?

What do you do?

What is your real name?

Those questions produced confusion.

The songs produced understanding.

Not complete understanding.

Nothing that dramatic.

But actual information.

Taste.

History.

Emotion.

Memory.

What somebody chooses when told:

> Introduce yourself with a song.

That is a hell of a question.

Much better than:

> Occupation?

## Names Are Useful

I am not anti-name.

Names are useful.

Eventually Moonflower told me hers.

Fine.

Now I know it.

And I still call her Moonflower.

Because by then that was who I had been talking to.

Maybe if I get to know her better, I will invent some other ridiculous nickname.

That happens.

Something occurs.

A detail becomes funny.

A name appears.

Then that name carries the event.

That feels more alive to me than insisting the government-issued identifier is somehow the deepest truth.

## Ask Anything

The next morning I sent her a picture of Bonnie.

She asked:

> Can I ask for the name?

Then:

> Or no?

Which made me laugh because she had already asked.

That was the whole fucking lesson from the night before in miniature.

You can always ask.

I can always answer.

Or not.

Those are separate permissions.

This time:

Bonnie.

Easy.

No psychological archive required.

## I Knew Her Before I Knew Her Name

That is the part I keep coming back to.

By the time I knew her actual name, I already knew things that mattered more to me.

I knew what made her laugh.

I knew something about her music.

I had heard her voice.

I knew she could throw a song back at me instead of making me carry the conversation alone.

I knew she could surprise me.

That was enough for the interaction to be real.

Maybe we keep talking.

Maybe we do not.

I have no idea.

But for two hours, Moonflower was enough.

And somehow that feels like a much better introduction than a form with:

Name.

Country.

Occupation.

Next.
