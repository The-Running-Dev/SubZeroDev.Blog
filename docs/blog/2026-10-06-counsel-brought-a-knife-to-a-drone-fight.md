# Your Honor, Counsel Brought a Knife to a Drone Fight

I have discovered a new recreational activity.

Destroying idioms.

<!-- truncate -->

Not accidentally.

Deliberately.

Everybody knows Rickisms from *Trailer Park Boys*:

Sayings that begin normally, take a catastrophic linguistic turn somewhere in the middle, and somehow still communicate exactly what was intended.

Wonderful.

But why stop there?

Why merely fuck up **one** idiom when you can crash several completely unrelated cultural references into each other and create something substantially worse?

For example:

> “Objection, Your Honor. Counsel brought a knife to a drone fight.”

There is absolutely no reason for that sentence to exist.

And yet you understand it immediately.

This pleases Lucifer.

## Controlled Idiom Collision

The basic formula is simple.

Take something everybody recognizes.

Then hit it with something else everybody recognizes.

Preferably at speed.

Traditional:

> “You brought a knife to a gunfight.”

Improved:

> “Counsel brought a knife to a drone fight.”

Now we have:

- courtroom procedure;
- an old idiom about being hopelessly outmatched;
- modern warfare;
- and the implication that opposing counsel has made several very poor technological decisions.

English survives.

Barely.

This opens possibilities.

> “Your Honor, this horse has been beaten past its warranty.”

The horse is dead.

The warranty is also apparently dead.

Customer support will not be taking your call.

Or:

> “We’ll burn that bridge when we get to it.”

That one already exists, and frankly humanity deserves credit.

It takes:

> “We’ll cross that bridge when we get to it.”

and:

> “Don’t burn your bridges.”

and produces the much more realistic strategic doctrine:

**We’ll deal with the consequences after we deliberately make them worse.**

Now *that* sounds like a plan.

## The Courtroom Expansion Pack

Lately Discount Store Alan Shore has apparently taken residence in my head.

This has introduced legal language into situations that have absolutely no business involving legal language.

Someone makes an unsupported claim:

> “Objection, Your Honor. Assumes facts not in evidence.”

Someone begins explaining their own personality instead of addressing the argument:

> “Objection, Your Honor. Counsel is testifying.”

Someone arrives spectacularly unprepared:

> “Your Honor, counsel brought a knife to a drone fight.”

Someone keeps moving the premise:

> “Counsel is moving the goalposts with a forklift.”

There is no judge.

There is no courtroom.

Nobody has retained counsel.

This does not appear to matter.

## May the Whatever Be With You

Then another disease developed.

Last night I was talking to someone while submitting a WinGet manifest.

The conversation ended.

Normal people might say:

> “Good night.”

Apparently I said:

> “May the PRs be with you.”

The next day she told me:

> “I may check on you in the evening.”

My brain immediately responded:

> “May the checks be with you.”

At this point the structure became reusable infrastructure.

Why write an original farewell ever again?

> “May the commits be with you.”

> “May the merges be with you.”

> “May the manifests be with you.”

> “May the trainings be with you.”

> “May the yogurt be with you.”

> “May the gargoyle be with you.”

> “May the evidence be with you.”

Eventually:

> “May the Universe be with you. Apparently.”

George Lucas spent decades building *Star Wars*.

I have reduced the Force to a string-template function.

Progress.

## This Is Not a Malapropism

The important part is that this is not simply getting a phrase wrong.

That would be too easy.

The point is that every component remains recognizable.

The listener should experience approximately three stages:

1. **Recognition:** “I know that phrase.”
2. **Confusion:** “Wait, that is not the phrase.”
3. **Acceptance:** “Unfortunately, I understand exactly what he means.”

That last part is essential.

If nobody understands it, you have merely said nonsense.

If everybody understands it despite the sentence having never previously existed, you have successfully abused the shared linguistic runtime.

This is engineering.

Probably.

## Rickisms Were the Prototype

Rickisms are still magnificent because they achieve something similar accidentally.

The language crashes halfway through the operation but somehow returns HTTP 200.

My version is more malicious.

I want the collision.

I want:

> “We’ll beat that dead horse when we get to the bridge.”

I want:

> “That ship has sailed under the bus.”

I want:

> “You opened that can of worms. Now lie in it.”

I want:

> “Your Honor, that argument is rearranging deck chairs on a burning dumpster.”

Some of these are terrible.

Excellent.

The terrible ones simply require further development.

## Lucifer Reviews the Specification

Lucifer looked over the growing list.

> “So you’re deliberately combining idioms now?”

“Yes.”

> “Movie quotes?”

“Yes.”

> “Courtroom language?”

“Obviously.”

> “Technical terminology?”

“Whenever possible.”

He stared at me.

> “And why?”

I considered this.

There were several possible answers.

Language evolves.

Idioms become stale.

Shared cultural references are building blocks.

Semantic expectations are merely another system available for abuse.

But those sounded unnecessarily serious.

So I gave him the correct answer.

> “Because I can.”

Lucifer nodded.

Fair enough.

Then he started walking away.

“Hey.”

He turned around.

I raised one hand solemnly.

> “May the idioms be with you.”

Lucifer closed his eyes.

Long pause.

> “You’re going to run this into the ground, aren’t you?”

I smiled.

> “We’ll burn that bridge when we get to it.”
