# I'm Not a Programmer

A woman on Tinder asked me if I was a programmer.

I said:

> “No.”

This was apparently misleading.

<!-- truncate -->

Later, in the same general interaction, we were discussing:

- React;
- Angular;
- Prototype;
- Scriptaculous;
- WinGet manifests;
- Chocolatey;
- SideQuest Easy Installer;
- public package manifests;
- and whatever other technical archaeology fell out of my head that evening.

At one point I was literally submitting a WinGet manifest while talking to her.

So from her side there was probably a reasonable question:

> “What exactly does this man think a programmer is?”

Easy.

A programmer is somebody who identifies as a programmer.

I don't.

I am a guy who programs.

That is not the same sentence.

## Activities Are Not Identities

I write code.

I also configure networks.

I fix computers.

I build infrastructure.

I work on CI pipelines.

I mess with Linux.

I mess with Windows.

I have built game-engine infrastructure, deployment tooling, automation, web applications, installers, services, and things that probably should not have existed but now do.

I have also repaired vehicles.

I have fixed electrical cables.

I have worked at a gas station.

I have cooked food.

I have driven motorcycles across countries.

None of this requires me to say:

> “This is what I am.”

It is what I do.

Sometimes.

There is a difference.

## The Identity Compression Problem

People love labels because labels compress information.

“What do you do?”

“Programmer.”

Done.

One word.

Very efficient.

Except the compression is lossy.

Ask me whether I program and the answer is obviously yes.

Ask me whether I am **a programmer** and now you are asking an identity question.

That is where I stop cooperating.

Because once the label lands, people begin attaching the rest of the package.

Programmer.

Therefore:

- probably sits at a desk all day;
- probably likes a certain kind of conversation;
- probably has a certain personality;
- probably has a certain career path;
- probably thinks about himself through technology.

No.

I write software because I know how to write software.

Sometimes because I am paid.

Sometimes because I need something.

Sometimes because something annoys me.

Sometimes because I looked at a problem and thought:

> “Fuck it. I can build that.”

That is not an identity.

That is a capability.

## Same With IT

I am also not an “IT guy.”

I do IT.

Sometimes absurd amounts of it.

I have had evenings where a television could not reach Plex and somehow, several hours later, I was deep inside DNS, Pi-hole, dnscrypt-proxy, VPN routing, network behavior, and undocumented Windows nonsense.

That does not mean I wake up every morning and think:

> “Ah yes. I am IT Man.”

I am just a guy who knows enough about these things to be dangerous.

Occasionally useful.

Frequently annoyed.

## The Tinder Problem

This is why the Tinder exchange was funny.

She asked:

> “Are you a programmer?”

I answered honestly:

> “No.”

Then later:

> “I’ve submitted a dozen manifests to the public repo.”

Then:

> “I was on the Chocolatey dev team once upon a time.”

Then:

> “I built one of those for a job interview.”

At no point did the original answer become false.

It merely became inconvenient.

The category she selected was too small.

## The Resume Is Not the Person

This probably creates problems.

Employers want a title.

Dating apps want categories.

Forms want a profession.

LinkedIn wants an identity stack.

Software Engineer.

Architect.

Technical Lead.

Staff Engineer.

Principal Engineer.

Head of Engineering.

Fine.

Use whatever label is operationally useful.

But internally?

No.

I am not the title.

I am not the stack.

I am not the activity.

I am not a programmer.

I am a guy who programs.

I am not an IT guy.

I am a guy who does IT.

Sometimes I am a guy who writes a blog post because one sentence on Tinder accidentally turned into a philosophical argument about identity.

Apparently I am also that.

For now.
