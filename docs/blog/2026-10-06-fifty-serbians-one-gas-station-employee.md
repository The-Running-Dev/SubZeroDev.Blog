# Fifty Serbians, One Gas Station Employee, and Van Diesel

I stopped for diesel on the way home.

The gas station was full.

Not normal full.

**Balkan migration event** full.

<!-- truncate -->

There were maybe fifty Serbians.

Going to Serbia.

Coming from Serbia.

Passing through.

I have no idea.

What mattered was that nearly all of them appeared to need the register at the same time.

And one poor bastard was behind it.

## The Register Scrum

If you have spent enough time in the Balkans, you know the scene.

There is technically a queue.

Philosophically.

Operationally, there are seventeen people leaning toward the same counter with the quiet conviction that their transaction is next.

Nobody is especially malicious.

Everybody simply believes forward movement is a personal responsibility.

The guy behind the register looked miserable.

Grumpy.

Irritated.

Overloaded.

I could see it on his face before I even got near him.

So I did something radical.

I sat down.

## Not Helping Is Sometimes Helping

I had already pumped the diesel.

Van Diesel was sitting at the pump.

Unpaid.

I could have joined the pile.

Instead I thought:

> “The guy is fucked. I can wait ten minutes.”

So I waited.

People paid.

People left.

The station slowly emptied.

For one brief moment I felt considerate.

Then I realized something.

My van was still at the pump.

The fuel was still unpaid.

The place was getting empty.

There was a non-zero chance the cashier was now thinking:

> “Did that motherfucker just steal diesel?”

Excellent.

I had attempted to reduce his stress and may have created a completely new category.

## I Almost Offered to Help

At one point I genuinely thought about asking whether I could help him.

I knew he probably would not take me up on it.

Register access.

Liability.

Rules.

Random customer suddenly becoming temporary staff.

Probably not.

But I had worked at a gas station once.

For about a month.

I knew enough to recognize the shape of the disaster.

So part of my brain went:

> “Can I take something off this guy's plate?”

The useful answer was probably no.

So I stayed seated.

## The Other Employees

Then I noticed something else.

There were other employees.

Two of them.

One was apparently supposed to pump gas.

I had pumped my own.

Another existed somewhere in the ecosystem.

Meanwhile the cashier was drowning.

I remember looking around and thinking:

> “What the fuck are those guys doing?”

Apparently this was not their problem either.

A familiar organizational architecture.

One person overloaded.

Several people nearby.

No load balancing.

Enterprise software has the same bug.

## The Shrug

Eventually I got to the counter.

I said something like:

> “Why did they leave you alone tonight? That must have sucked.”

He shrugged.

That was it.

No speech.

No rant.

No complaint.

Just the full Balkan shrug.

The universal compression algorithm for:

**Yes. Obviously. What do you want me to do about it?**

Entire paragraphs disappeared into two shoulders.

## I Have Been Behind That Counter

The scene probably landed differently because I have done that job.

Years ago, after enough family pressure about employment, I took a gas-station job.

I had a college degree.

I was standing behind a gas-station counter wondering:

> “Why the fuck am I working here?”

I lasted about a month.

Long enough to know that when fifty people converge on the register, the person behind it is not secretly enjoying the challenge.

Long enough to know that one more impatient customer is not motivational.

## Van Diesel Pays His Debts

Eventually I paid.

Nobody accused me of stealing fuel.

Van Diesel survived another outing.

The Serbian crowd moved on.

The cashier remained employed.

The other employees presumably continued whatever mysterious side quest had prevented them from helping.

And I drove home thinking that occasionally the most useful social contribution available is extremely simple:

See the person drowning.

Do not become another bucket of water.

Sit the fuck down for ten minutes.
