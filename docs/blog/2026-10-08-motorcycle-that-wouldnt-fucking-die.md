# The Motorcycle That Wouldn't Fucking Die

My ex-wife once got tired of me staring at every motorcycle that drove past.

"Why don't you just buy a bike already?"

So around 2007, I did. Brand-new, 1000cc. A considerable step up from the battered 1980s Yamaha I'd been riding.

Nineteen years later, in 2026, the thing finally died.

Somewhere between those dates, the bike acquired more international history than most people.

<!-- truncate -->

## Texas: First Blood

A friend who had never ridden a motorcycle decided to try my thousand-cc bike. He barely made it through the gate of the apartment complex before going straight instead of turning and dropping it.

Busted plastics. Broken stand. Not the frame.

He felt awful, helped replace the parts, and the bike carried on.

A few years later, as I prepared to leave Texas in 2018, I sold the Mitsubishi Evo and decided to take the motorcycle to Europe instead.

This seemed sensible at the time.

## Bremerhaven, Germany

I put it on a container ship from Texas. It arrived in Bremerhaven. I went to collect it.

Germany wouldn't let me out of the port without buying insurance on the spot. I also paid about €1,200 in import tax, later refunded when the bike made it to Bulgaria as my own vehicle.

And I didn't truck it from Germany to Bulgaria.

I fucking rode it.

## Southampton: Missing Motorcycle

Later, I took it to the UK, loaded with so much luggage it looked like a pack mule. Somewhere on a Belgian highway I stepped off, the bike tipped, and I spent roughly an hour lifting it and reconstructing the luggage arrangement. Nobody stopped.

I made it to Southampton, lived there for a while, and once made the mistake of leaving the motorcycle unlocked overnight.

Next morning: no bike.

I kept looking at the empty space, as if a missing thousand-cc motorcycle might spontaneously reappear.

I posted a £200 reward online. Some Polish kids surfaced with information and the motorcycle came back. I suspected they were the thieves; I didn't prove it. I paid the reward.

Someone in Southampton repaired the damage badly. Years later, that repair would send me to a tow truck near Nice.

## Barcelona and the Rest of Europe

When I moved to Barcelona, the bike initially remained behind. Eventually I returned to Southampton, collected it in September, put on what amounted to an Eskimo suit, and rode through cold France to Spain.

It lived with me in Spain for years. I rode between Spain and Bulgaria more than once.

At one point I put diesel in it in the mountains near Girona. It stopped a couple of kilometers later. I walked toward a gas station with a discarded plastic bottle, and an older Spanish couple turned around to pick me up. They didn't speak English; my Spanish was limited. My card failed, so the gentleman handed me €5, drove me back, and I got the bike going again.

This was extremely useful knowledge when I [put diesel in it again, this time in Greece](/motorcycle-diplomacy/).

Another trip began as Sofia → Greece → overnight ferry to Italy → ferry from Civitavecchia to Barcelona.

That plan disintegrated when my documents flew out of an open pocket. Customs refused to let me board with photographs of my ID. I cried outside the port at 1 a.m., slept in an Italian village, then simply rode north through Italy, across southern France, and toward Valencia.

The bad Southampton electrical repair finally killed the bike near Nice. €300 tow, €30 repair, €50 paid out of gratitude. Then back on the road.

[The Ferry That Never Happened](/the-ferry-that-never-happened/) tells the full mess.

## Ben the Legend

In Barcelona I joined a motorcycle riding group. Around twenty of us rode the mountain roads on weekends.

We took an overnight ferry to Mallorca. I'd barely slept, hadn't been riding enough, and the bike wasn't in great shape. At the first curve outside Palma it went straight into gravel, rotated violently, and went down.

The front of the bike was damaged. My left knee was fucked. I couldn't walk properly for a couple of months.

As soon as I could walk, I rode with the group again. Slowly. So slowly the rider assigned to keep the group together had to keep waiting for me.

The group leader called me **Ben the Legend**.

I hadn't appointed myself anything.

The full incident is in [Ben the Legend](/ben-the-legend/).

## Bulgarian Mechanical Diplomacy

The motorcycle was American-spec, with a miles speedometer. For years I drove it around Bulgaria with American plates—Texas and New York plates both existed in my collection. I had no Bulgarian license, local insurance, or Bulgarian registration for the bike at the time.

Police stopped me repeatedly. I kept receiving different explanations.

Somewhere around 2018 or 2019, a loose chain slapped the engine case until it made a hole. I got it to Plovdiv, had the damaged case welded, and kept riding.

If it moved, the story wasn't finished.

## Nineteen Years

It was dropped, stolen, rescued, fueled incorrectly twice, crashed, shipped, ferried, towed, welded, repaired, ridden across countries, and covered in luggage.

Eventually it died.

I don't have to pretend this is a legend about human endurance. It's the history of a motorcycle I bought because somebody said, "Why don't you just buy one?"

And I did.
