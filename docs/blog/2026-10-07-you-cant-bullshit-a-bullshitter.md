# You Can't Bullshit a Bullshitter

Someone tells me she likes pushing people's buttons.

She studies human behavior.

Interesting.

I used to push buttons too.

At the time I could also have supplied a fairly impressive explanation for why I was doing it.

Looking back, the explanation could have been considerably shorter.

I enjoyed it.

<!-- truncate -->

## Research With a Convenient Reward

There is something entertaining about discovering exactly which sentence makes somebody react.

You say the thing.

Something happens.

There it is.

The button works.

Then you can stand back and explain the reaction.

Very analytical.

Very observant.

Also, in my case, rather conveniently arranged so I got to provoke someone and then treat their response as the interesting part.

My own contribution could disappear into the footnotes.

I wasn't simply observing the behavior.

I was enjoying my ability to produce it.

Calling that an interest in people did not make the enjoyment disappear.

It just gave it better stationery.

## I Remember the Appeal

I don't need to pretend the younger version of me was some incomprehensible stranger.

I remember why it was fun.

I could get a reaction.

I could demonstrate that I'd noticed something.

I could feel clever.

Sometimes I had something to prove, and a reaction supplied the evidence.

There.

See?

I can do that.

Eventually that became less interesting to me.

Getting someone to react stopped feeling like much of an achievement.

Yes, Ben.

You found a button.

There are quite a lot of them.

What exactly did we win?

## A Brief Opening

During this conversation, she briefly entertained the possibility that she might need to grow up.

That was the interesting moment.

A little room appeared around the behavior.

Maybe this thing I do isn't the whole of who I am.

Maybe I could look at it.

Then came the harder statements.

Nothing to prove.

Doesn't care about people.

That movement caught my attention.

A moment of questioning, followed by a much firmer description of the person doing the questioning.

I recognized it.

I've had my own versions of opening the door a little, noticing what is outside, and rapidly remembering that the door was supposed to be locked.

## The Explanation Has a Job

The useful part of recognizing an old habit is remembering what the explanation used to do for me.

It let me continue.

If I was simply studying people, I didn't have to spend very long on the fact that I enjoyed winding them up.

If I had nothing to prove, there was no need to examine why proving the point felt so satisfying.

The account of myself arrived with the uncomfortable part already edited out.

Very efficient.

Also bullshit.

I can recognize that arrangement without needing to turn the conversation into a trial.

The recognition is enough to make me say what I see.

It doesn't appoint me to supervise somebody else's development.

## When It's Time

I told her, in effect, when it's time, it'll be time.

I don't remember a lecture being the thing that made provoking people less rewarding for me.

The reward changed.

Or I did.

The thing I was getting from it stopped being something I particularly needed.

I can still be absurd.

I can still say something blunt.

I can still enjoy a ridiculous exchange.

But producing a reaction isn't automatically the prize anymore.

I don't need to make somebody uncomfortable just to confirm that I know how.

## Familiar Machinery

That's what I mean by you can't bullshit a bullshitter.

Sometimes the performance is familiar because I remember performing it.

The explanation.

The little flash of doubt.

The quick return to a harder version of myself.

I know how persuasive that story can sound from inside it.

I used to be the one telling it.

Which makes the most useful part of this conversation less about catching somebody else out than remembering something about myself.

I once thought being able to push the button proved something.

These days I can notice the button and keep having the conversation.
