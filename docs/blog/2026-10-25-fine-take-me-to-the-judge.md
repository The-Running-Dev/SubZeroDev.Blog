# Fine Take Me to the Judge

I need to write down some of my police and customs encounters because they're fucking absurd. Talking about the house brought several of them back: the demands, the threats, and the expectation that I would get frightened.

<!-- truncate -->

## Ten Dollars and a Passport Held for Whisky

Bulgaria, 2001. I had a U.S. passport and lived in the States.

A customs officer took my passport and told me to buy him a bottle of whisky or he would not give it back.

That was the demand. Go shopping for him if I wanted my own fucking passport returned.

I had ten dollars in my pocket. That was the practical problem. Ten dollars is not much of a whisky budget. I bought the cheapest bottle I could and gave it to him.

Afterwards I regretted not thinking of a different ending: take the bottle into the bathroom, piss in it and hand it over. I didn't do that. It was the revenge idea that came later, and I still find it darkly funny.

The actual story is less satisfying. He had my passport. I had very little money. I spent some of it buying him a drink to get back something that was already mine.

I remember the sheer directness of it. No subtle hint. Buy me whisky, or you do not get your passport.

That's one of my memories of how Bulgarian customs used to operate. A bottle of cheap whisky and a U.S. passport, with a man behind a desk deciding the first was the price of returning the second.

## The Radar Detector and the Threat of a Judge

I had been using that radar detector for years. At Serbian customs they took it. I received no paperwork for it.

I challenged them. Give it back. Explain what you are doing.

The answer became a threat to take me before a judge. There was shouting. I didn't shout back.

Fine. Take me to the judge.

I was willing to go through the process they kept threatening. If that was what came next, let us do it. I was not interested in being frightened into dropping the question.

I believed the confiscation was unjustified. I was not using the detector in Serbia, and I wanted them to explain the basis for taking it. Their volume did not answer that.

One of them also questioned my arm-mounted phone holder as though it were a pistol holster. That detail did very little to improve my impression of the encounter.

They took the detector and left the dedicated power supply. That was the absurd little consolation. I could buy another detector. Whoever had this one would still need a way to power it.

I remember thinking they could plug it up their arse and see whether that charged it. Not something I did, obviously. Just the sort of thought the encounter deserved.

It was annoying, but the device itself was replaceable. What stayed with me was being expected to accept the taking of my property without an explanation, and then being threatened when I asked for one.

## Coffee Money Away From the Cameras

Another Serbian customs encounter, around the COVID period. I'm not pinning an exact year on this recollection.

They inspected my luggage. There was Jack Daniel’s Honey in it, possibly two bottles I was bringing over. The officer claimed I could not bring two bottles of whisky across the border.

Fine. Take one.

He did not take the bottle.

Instead he wanted money for coffee. I reached into my pocket and took out twenty euros.

No, not there. There were cameras.

I went inside and handed him the money. He let me go.

That's the part of the story I remember particularly clearly: the direction to move the payment away from the cameras. We were not left trying to interpret a vague social invitation to have a coffee together.

I believed the whisky restriction was a pretext. I offered the very object he claimed was the problem, and he did not want it. The bottles remained with me. Twenty euros did not.

When I first retold this I mixed up “whisky money” and “coffee money.” The demand in this encounter was for coffee money; the whisky was what he had found in the luggage and used as his objection.

A luggage inspection, a complaint about a bottle, and then instructions on where to hand over cash without being recorded. That was the encounter.

## Take the Car and Let Me Off the Highway

On the Belgrade highway there was an encounter over my car smoking too much.

It became a discussion about taking the car or the plates. The car was not in my name. The documents were Bulgarian. I was on my way somewhere and had my bag.

All right. Take the fucking car. Take the plates.

What I wanted at that point was to get off the highway and go about my business. I was not going to stand there treating the car as an object I would do anything to preserve.

I remember the hard manner and the threats about consequences. I also remember how practical my own answer felt to me. If that was what they were going to do, then do it and let me work out the next part.

I got off the highway and carried on.

The memorable part was the mismatch. They were talking about the car as a frightening consequence. I was asking how I could leave the highway.

## The Warning About Tinted Windows

Someone once threatened to take my car or plates over tinted windows.

My answer was simple. Fine. Take them.

Then it became a warning.

I hadn't pleaded for one. I hadn't argued, shouted or tried to bargain my way out of anything. I had accepted the consequence he said he intended to impose.

That made the announcement of the warning funny to me. There was a buildup towards something I was apparently meant to fear, and then a reprieve I hadn't asked for.

This is how I describe many of these encounters: I don't start by getting defensive or verbally aggressive. Sometimes I ask how the officer’s day is going or whether I can buy him a coffee. I speak to the person standing there.

I don't know what each officer is thinking. I know that I'm not obliged to supply the fear somebody might expect to see on my face.

With the tinted windows, the outcome was a warning. My answer before that outcome was still: if you are going to take the plates, take them.

## Order Another Licence Then

Four police cars and eight officers. They took my U.S. licence, and I received no paperwork for it.

There were statements about taking the car, taking the licence and all the rest. The car was not in my name. I was sitting there laughing at the absurdity of the scene.

I told one of them I would order another licence the next day.

He looked furious to me.

Order one, then.

I did.

Two of the younger officers asked why I was acting like a clown and told me this was not funny. It was funny to me, and I laughed more.

That pissed me off. There was whatever they were doing about the car and licence, and then there was the question of how they thought I should behave while they did it.

I was prepared to face consequences. I was not volunteering to look frightened, solemn or suitably chastened for their benefit.

I was also looking at men much younger than me telling me how to conduct myself because they were wearing uniforms. I didn't regard that as personal superiority.

The exchange about the replacement licence is the part that still makes me laugh. I told him what I intended to do. He told me to do it. I did.

## Why Are You Hiding in Your Own House

Elizabeth once called the police for a wellness check. She knew where I lived. It was about a ten-minute walk away, and she could have come herself. I was angry that she had chosen to involve the police instead.

I was at home drinking and getting high. For perhaps half an hour there were sirens and noise outside. I went onto the balcony, wondered what the fuck was going on, looked around and went back inside. I didn't know the activity was about me.

Eventually I went out, and an officer asked why I was hiding from them.

Hiding? I was in my own house.

The question arrived with an intention already attached to me. I hadn't seen a commotion outside and decided to conceal myself from a police operation. I had seen a commotion outside and gone back indoors.

He started giving me a lecture. I wanted him to get to the point.

Enough with this bullshit. Tell me why you are here so we can deal with it.

I wanted the purpose of the visit explained. The encounter had started with me answering for supposedly hiding before anybody had made clear why they were looking for me.

That's the scene I remember: me at home, noise outside, and then an accusation about an intention I had never had.

## I Told the Investigator When I Would Be There

A police investigator called about me attending an appointment connected with a subpoena. I asked what it was about and said the proposed timing did not work. Let us arrange a time.

The conversation became: I can make you come. I can send people over.

Then make me.

People were sent and did not find me. That did not turn the threats into a useful explanation of what she wanted.

I thought she was handling the exchange badly. She was younger than me and had a position of authority, but I didn't accept that this made her personally superior to me or entitled to speak as though she owned my time.

I did go. The detail matters: I told her exactly when I would be there. Tomorrow, at this hour. I let her think what she liked about who had controlled the exchange while giving her the actual time myself.

I saw going as the more mature choice. I didn't want to keep measuring dicks over an appointment.

Showing up did not mean that I felt intimidated. I decided to get the matter handled, and I still thought her manner was disrespectful.

My request had been straightforward: tell me what this concerns and let us arrange when I can attend. We managed to turn that into a confrontation before I even got there.

## The Election Day Drinking Ban at the Bar

I was at the bar and found out that apparently it was election day and the mayor had forbidden drinking in the town.

That's what I was told at the bar.

The bartender had forgotten. I had been served a drink anyway.

The scene struck me as absurd. Somebody apparently decides that people in the town are not drinking today, and at the place serving drinks the rule has already slipped somebody’s mind.

It caught me in the middle of a day spent talking about cupboards, family authority, police encounters and what people think they can tell other people to do. Now there was another instruction, supposedly applying to everyone in town for the day.

I was angry about it, and I found the gap between the announcement and the actual scene in the bar funny.

I don't have a grand ending to this one. I heard about a drinking ban while sitting with the drink the bartender had already served me.
