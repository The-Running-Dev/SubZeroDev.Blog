# You Asked Me a Question and Then Got Mad at the Answer

There is a recurring social experiment I keep accidentally participating in.

Someone asks me a question.

I answer it.

They keep asking.

I keep answering.

Eventually they seem surprised that the answers are complicated.

<!-- truncate -->

This is especially funny in dating.

The profiles are not subtle.

I am not presenting myself as:

> “Normal man. Enjoys brunch. Seeking normal woman for normal activities.”

The weirdness is visible.

The absurdity is visible.

The unconventional life is visible.

At one point I literally told a woman:

> “I have an absurd personality.”

Her response was basically:

> “You don't say.”

Excellent.

Disclosure complete.

Then later, when I behave like the same person described in the profile, there can still be this strange moment of:

> “Oh.”

Yes.

Oh.

## You Asked

Where are you from?

Complicated.

What do you do?

Also complicated.

How long have you lived here?

Which “here”?

Why did you move?

How much time do you have?

These are not evasions.

They are accurate warnings.

I have lived in multiple countries.

Worked across multiple technical domains.

Had multiple careers inside one career.

Driven and abandoned and repaired vehicles in multiple places.

Built things.

Broken things.

Moved.

Returned.

Changed direction.

Changed direction again.

If you keep asking questions, eventually the answer is going to stop fitting in a neat little box.

That is not intensity.

That is information.

## The Curiosity Trap

The part I find funny is when somebody pushes into the detail.

They ask the second question.

Then the third.

Then the fourth.

They are actively pulling the thread.

Fine.

I like conversation.

Pull.

But sometimes, after enough thread has been pulled, the reaction becomes:

> “That's a lot.”

Correct.

You asked for the long version.

There was always a short version available.

You did not select it.

## “Too Much”

I understand the phrase.

Sometimes people mean:

- too much history;
- too much energy;
- too much directness;
- too much information;
- too many stories;
- too much intensity;
- too much difference from what they expected.

Fine.

People are allowed to want whatever they want.

What amuses me is when the person actively excavated the material and then reacts as though I dumped it on the table unprompted.

No.

You brought a shovel.

## Product Description

This is where dating profiles become comedy.

People say they want:

- unusual;
- interesting;
- adventurous;
- intelligent;
- unconventional;
- authentic;
- different.

Then sometimes they meet “different” and discover they meant:

**different, but still inside a comfortable range of familiar behavior.**

That is fair too.

But it is a different product.

I do not think I have been advertising the standard model.

The product description says absurd.

The product behaves absurdly.

Customer service cannot issue a refund because the unit matches specification.

## The Better Question

Maybe the useful question is not:

> “Why are you like this?”

Maybe it is:

> “Do I actually enjoy this person as he is?”

That is much simpler.

No diagnosis.

No optimization.

No project.

No attempt to reduce an unconventional life into a category that makes it easier to process.

Just:

Do you like it?

If yes, cool.

If no, also cool.

But if you ask me where I am from, what I do, why I moved, what I built, why I think the way I do, and how the hell I ended up here—

please do not be shocked when the answer turns out to contain more than three bullet points.

You asked.
