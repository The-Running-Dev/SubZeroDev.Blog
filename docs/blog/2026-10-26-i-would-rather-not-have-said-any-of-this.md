# I Would Rather Not Have Said Any of This

I finished a letter to my younger brother today. The subject was: “I said I will get back to you, I would have rather not said any of this.” That is about as accurate a description of this whole exercise as I can give.

<!-- truncate -->

His email arrived on January 22, 2025. It is now October 2026. I have written versions of the reply three or four times. I kept leaving them unsent.

I did not spend that time searching for permission to defend myself. I did not think I owed a defence of my character just because someone had reached conclusions about it. Silence seemed like the best response available.

But silence did not mean I had nothing to say.

There was a lot to say. Apparently several thousand fucking words of it.

## The responsibility that was already there

There are three sons in my family. I am the oldest, roughly ten years older than my youngest brother.

When he came home as a baby, I was ten. Responsibility for younger brothers became part of my childhood while I was still a child myself. I remember traveling across the country alone at ten to twelve, buying alcohol for our parents at ten, and being beaten when my middle brother did something wrong.

Later, when our parents separated, I was twenty-five and my youngest brother was fifteen. I was trying to maintain relationships with both parents while each spoke against the other. I loved both of them. Neither seemed particularly interested in how it felt to be the person receiving both accounts.

Helping continued into adulthood. My middle brother and I spent hours typing our mother's long medical papers while she was in nursing school. There were errands, driving, money, repairs, and the practical work that disappears from a family story because somebody always did it.

I helped because she was my mother. I believed helping her was the thing to do.

I wasn't secretly negotiating a reward. I wasn't building a spreadsheet of favours that would mature into a payout thirty years later. I was doing things that needed doing.

And being the oldest did not make that a permanent assignment.

## Spain was supposed to be a plan

I had lived in Spain for five years. I hoped to stay.

I brought my mother there because she was getting older and I thought we could work out a way to live near each other. I could be around. We could make something workable.

I had earned money and supported myself, but I did not have enough capital to buy a home. I had last owned one about fifteen years earlier and had spent the intervening years moving between rentals.

Our arrangement was that she would provide the purchase money upfront. I would get a job, contribute, and repay at least half.

That was the plan. Not a fantasy I had invented without talking to her.

We saw around thirty houses. There was a lease ending in roughly a month or two. She would like a house one day and change her mind the next. This went on for weeks.

She was entitled to hesitate about a purchase. We also had a deadline. Trying to move an agreed plan forward became, in her account, me pressuring her.

At one point, after the breakdown of her relationship with my middle brother, she said to my face:

> “I can lose another son.”

I heard that as a way to hurt me and make me feel guilty. I remember not knowing what to say.

Eventually I said I could not keep living with her under those conditions. We agreed to take time apart. She said she would find a room in Spain. I went to Bulgaria for two weeks.

I returned intending to explore another arrangement. Separate apartments, perhaps. Nearby, but not under the same roof. I had not worked out every detail. I was still trying.

When I returned, she was gone.

There was a letter.

She and my younger brother had arranged for her to return to America and live with him. Neither had called me before making that arrangement.

Later, his email described him as forced to restructure the next ten to fifteen years of his life, while I had apparently left town and gone on living mine.

That was not what I had gone away to do.

## An account I did not recognize

What troubled me about his email was the confidence with which it explained me.

It questioned my integrity, interpreted my motives, and described roles in the family that did not match what I remembered. He and I had not had a conversation about the events in Spain.

One incident illustrates the gap.

We went to the beach. After an argument, I said I was leaving and asked whether she wanted to come with me. She declined. I came back and asked again. She declined again.

I left.

The later account was that I had abandoned her. The offers and refusals were missing. My brother never asked me about that incident.

I could accept a conversation about what I actually did. I could answer questions. I wanted my assumptions challenged when I revisited the email, too. But I kept encountering conclusions reached before my account had entered the conversation.

His email said he did not want to know what either side had done to the other. It also told me I needed to speak and live truthfully.

That combination is still difficult for me to understand.

How do you decline the history and remain certain about the judgment?

## Apparently help needs a purity test

He asked whether I would accept things from our mother if I had my own means. He suggested that accepting benefits while speaking as I did placed convenience above righteousness.

I already had my own means. I had worked, supported myself, moved countries, and helped members of the family.

That did not mean I could purchase a house outright. It also did not mean I had signed a declaration promising never to accept help from anybody.

I could contribute to a place and benefit from living there. My mother could receive the work and benefit from it too. Those things were not contradictory.

Why did the relationship suddenly need to be divided into the person giving and the person taking, as though nobody could do both?

My brother had also received substantial family support, including help with his house. I did not object to him receiving it. I objected to a standard that scrutinized my acceptance as a moral problem without the same examination of his.

The same confusion appeared around future care. We had discussed contributing when significant care became necessary: nurses, a care home, or something comparable. That discussion did not erase the years of practical care I was already providing.

There was a great deal of attention to what I would no longer do. Much less to what I had already done.

## I asked for a conversation

Before the move to Spain became difficult, I told my brother it would be hard and that I would need support.

Not money.

A phone call.

There are things I find easy to give and difficult to ask for. Help is one of them. Growing up, I learned to figure things out while also carrying responsibilities for other people. Asking somebody to be there does not come naturally to me.

When I was struggling, I tried to express something frightening: the thought of taking my own life and causing him that loss mattered deeply to me.

His email understood that conversation as making him my reason to live and placing responsibility for my life on him.

That was not what I was asking.

I was not asking him to keep me alive or become my therapist. I needed to say something difficult to my brother and be heard. Acknowledgment. A conversation. Nothing he had to solve.

I can understand that hearing someone you love speak about suicide is frightening. But the meaning I was trying to communicate was not an assignment to become my savior.

When I revisited his email, I kept returning to that distinction.

I had needed someone to listen. What came back was an explanation of why listening meant I had made him responsible.

## Admiration was not an appointment

I admired qualities in my brother. His ease with people, particularly. We could go somewhere and he could talk to anybody.

I liked that about him. I wished I could do it as easily. Over time, I found my own way of connecting with people.

His email described my admiration as pressure and questioned whether it was appropriate for an older brother to look up to a younger one. It also said he would prefer a friend he could aspire to be like.

I still do not understand why admiration became acceptable in one direction and a burden in the other.

I did not think he was above me. I did not think he was beneath me. I admired something he did well.

When he needed somewhere to live after graduating, he stayed with me in Texas for six months without paying rent. I did not use that help to claim authority over his future. I would have liked him to stay in Texas. His decision remained his.

He had also been there for me. I remember him dropping everything and coming with me to El Paso.

I did not want either memory turned into an invoice. I wanted the relationship they belonged to acknowledged.

## Whose childhood was being described?

The divorce passage was another place where I stopped.

He described not being allowed to be a child because he had to become emotionally mature and make everybody comfortable.

I am not claiming his childhood was easy or that I know every feeling he had. I do remember being there.

I remember trying to maintain contact with both parents. I remember the responsibilities I carried. I remember stepping into a house full of teenagers drinking and taking their keys and alcohol because no parent was there handling it.

He did not have a relationship with our father for years.

When he described himself as the person who had to keep everybody comfortable, I wanted to know which responsibilities he meant. What situations? Whom was he managing?

That is a question about the history, not a claim that I own the right to have had a difficult childhood.

Mine had disappeared from the explanation of why supporting me had become too much.

## Moving forward was not false hope

His email also said I was leading our mother on by remaining in ordinary contact with her.

But I had repeatedly tried to reconcile. I had said that we were both hurting, that we had both made mistakes, and that I wanted us to get along.

Sometimes the attempt was no more ambitious than a conversation or a meal.

What he called business as usual was me trying to stop reliving the same conflict. I had explained my grievances many times. Repeating them had not resolved them.

I wanted a basic relationship without pretending that the previous arrangement could simply resume.

There were later attempts too. Some became arguments about money, previous promises, or even what name I was allowed to use. But the fact that they broke down did not make the attempts dishonest.

I was not maintaining false hope for an advantage. I was trying to find something we could still have.

## Nearly two years of “some time apart”

In June 2025, I replied briefly. I told my brother I loved him as my brother, my equal, my friend, and my trusted ally. I said I would address the email eventually.

In March 2026, I reached out to both brothers with a different proposal.

We had enough family drama. Could we spend some time together now? Play something online. Watch videos. Share ideas. Talk nonsense. Find an hour that worked.

I did not require anyone to accept my account of the past first.

My younger brother declined. He said he did not have the emotional capacity, was concentrating on school, and that perhaps we could try after graduation, more than a year away.

I understand that my invitation did not resolve every grievance. It was still an invitation.

By October, the original request for some time apart was approaching two years. There had been words about reconsidering and reconnecting. There had not been a return.

## Writing the answer

I came back to the email because I wanted to question my own account. I did not want an exercise where every sentence I said came back to me as agreement.

I asked to be challenged. One point at a time, because fifteen objections at once were not helping me examine anything.

Some distinctions mattered. What I intended and what somebody heard were not necessarily identical. Someone's private experience could not be disproved simply by listing the freedoms they had. A difficult period in a relationship was not necessarily its whole history.

Those distinctions did not remove the events I remembered. They did not turn an arrangement made without me into a decision I had made. They did not make a request for conversation into a request for somebody else to run my life.

The first reply I wrote was full of insults. I meant the substance underneath them. Eventually I removed the insults and kept working through the claims, paragraph by paragraph.

The finished letter used a simple format: his words, then my reply.

I did not want to keep polishing it until it sounded like a different person had lived the story. I wanted to say what I meant clearly and stop.

## Words I have tried to practice

My brother once told our other brother that love is a verb.

I hear the language. What matters to me is what follows it.

My practice of Stoicism, Buddhism, and Zen has become an effort to accept what I cannot change, remain present, and keep learning. I am not announcing that I have mastered any of it. I am trying to live it.

I cannot change what happened in Spain. I cannot rewrite our childhood. I cannot make my brother reconsider his account of me.

I can reach out. I did.

I can say that I want us to be brothers without requiring every old argument to be settled first. I did that too.

I cannot erase the hurt. I also do not believe we must resolve every disagreement before we are allowed to share an hour together. Some things might be worked through over time. Some might remain unresolved.

That was what I meant by leaving the past behind. I was not asking for historical amnesia. I was offering a relationship in the present.

I finished the reply today. There is no tidy reconciliation to report. The letter does not make anybody understand anything, and writing it does not settle what happens next.

But I have said what I believe, as clearly as I could.

The subject was not a declaration of triumph. I would rather not have needed to say any of it.

I wanted a conversation with my brother.

Those were my words. Reaching out was my action.
